Bubble wrap and a moving blanket work fine for most of a household. They are not a plan for a painting, an inherited armoire, or anything you'd call irreplaceable.

## Fine Art and Antiques Aren't "Fragile Items"

Most packing guides lump "fragile" into one bucket: dishes, glassware, lamps, picture frames. That works for things you can replace at a store if they crack. It falls apart the moment you're dealing with a piece that has no replacement, only a repair, an appraisal, and a claim.

The difference matters because the whole plan changes. A cracked wine glass is a minor loss. A hairline split in a 90-year-old veneer dresser, or a flaking corner on an oil painting, isn't something a mover can just replace off a shelf. That changes how you pack it, how you insure it, and who you trust to carry it down the stairs. [General fragile-item packing](/blog/packing-fragile-items-boston-move) still applies to your everyday glassware and frames. This is about the pieces that sit above that line.

## Does Your Homeowners Insurance Actually Cover This?

Most people assume their homeowners or renters policy has this handled. It doesn't, not fully.

Standard policies typically still cover named perils during a move, meaning theft, fire, or an accident involving the moving truck itself. What they generally exclude is simple breakage or damage that happens during ordinary handling, which is the most common way art and antiques actually get hurt in transit. The [Insurance Information Institute](https://www.iii.org/article/getting-right-insurance-coverage-moving) advises checking your policy's specifics before a move for exactly this reason, and for high-value items, adding a scheduled personal property endorsement rather than assuming your base policy has you covered.

That gap is the whole reason mover valuation coverage exists. It's not a redundant add-on. It's the coverage built for the scenario your home policy skips.

## The Real Math Once You're Covering a $15,000 Painting

We [cover declared value versus released value in depth elsewhere](/blog/moving-insurance-boston), but here's the version that matters for anything genuinely valuable.

Released Value Protection is the free default, and it pays $0.60 per pound, per item. A 12-pound framed painting worth $15,000 nets you about $7.20 if something happens to it. That number isn't a typo, and it's not negotiable after the fact. It's the baseline coverage every mover has to offer, and most people don't realize how little it actually protects until something goes wrong.

Full Value Protection is the one you want for anything irreplaceable. You declare a value, the mover charges a premium based on it, and if the piece is damaged, they owe you a repair, a like-kind replacement, or the declared cash value. The premium is a real cost, but it's a rounding error next to the alternative of eating the loss on a piece you can't get back.

| Coverage Type | What It Actually Pays | Best For |
|---|---|---|
| Released Value Protection (default, free) | $0.60 per pound, per item | Boxes, everyday furniture, nothing irreplaceable |
| Full Value Protection (declared, paid) | Repair, like-kind replacement, or declared cash value | Anything you'd genuinely miss |
| Homeowners/Renters Insurance | Named perils only — theft, fire, transport accident | A backstop, not your primary coverage |
| Standalone Fine Art/Transit Policy | Scheduled, itemized coverage per named piece | Appraised art, documented antique collections |

## When Should You Get the Piece Appraised?

Before you book the move, not after something happens to it.

An appraisal from a qualified personal-property appraiser establishes what the [Appraisers Association of America](https://www.appraisersassociation.org) calls Retail Replacement Value: the cost to replace the item with one of comparable age, quality, and provenance within a reasonable time frame. That's the number that should go on your declared-value paperwork, not what you paid decades ago and not a guess at resale value, which tends to run lower than what it would actually cost you to replace the piece.

If you inherited something and have no idea what it's worth, that uncertainty is itself a reason to get it looked at before moving day. You can't accurately insure a number you're guessing at, and "it's probably fine" is not a coverage strategy for a piece that's been in the family for three generations.

## Custom Crate or Moving Blanket? How to Tell

Not everything needs a crate, and over-crating a whole house just burns budget you didn't need to spend.

Glass, marble tops, gilded or ornate frames, anything with an existing crack or a loose joint, and pieces with delicate inlay or veneer generally need a rigid custom crate built to the item's exact dimensions. The crate isn't about looking impressive on the truck — it's there to stop the piece from shifting, flexing, or taking a direct hit if something else in the load moves.

Solid, structurally sound furniture without fragile surfaces, like a sturdy dresser or a dining table without a glass or marble top, usually travels fine wrapped in furniture pads and secured with straps, the same way we handle [pianos, safes, and other heavy specialty pieces](/blog/moving-pianos-safes-pool-tables-boston). The test is simple: if the piece could take a moderate bump on a flat surface without damage, a blanket is enough. If a moderate bump would crack, chip, or shatter something, it needs a crate.

## Temperature and Humidity Do More Damage Than Bad Handling

This is the part general movers and even careful families miss most often. A piece can survive the truck ride physically intact and still come out damaged, because of what the air did to it, not the road.

Wood veneer, canvas, and paper all expand and contract with temperature and humidity swings. The [Northeast Document Conservation Center](https://www.nedcc.org/free-resources/preservation-leaflets/2.-the-environment/2.1-temperature,-relative-humidity,-light,-and-air-quality-basic-guidelines-for-preservation), a New England-based conservation authority, notes that gradual, stable conditions matter more than hitting an exact number, and that sudden swings are what actually cause cracking, warping, and flaking in art and antiques. A piece moving straight from a climate-controlled condo into a closed truck sitting in August heat or a January cold snap for a few hours is exactly the kind of swing that causes damage days after the move, once you've already stopped worrying about it.

If a piece is genuinely valuable, ask whether the truck has any climate control, and avoid letting art or veneer furniture sit in a hot or freezing vehicle overnight between pickup and delivery.

## Getting Antiques Down a Triple-Decker Stairwell

Boston's housing stock makes this section necessary in a way most cities don't. A huge share of the city's apartments sit in triple-deckers and pre-war walk-ups with narrow stairwells, tight landing turns, and zero margin for error carrying something wide, heavy, or awkward.

A crated painting is usually manageable. A large armoire, a marble-top buffet, or a grandfather clock is a different problem entirely, and [furniture that won't clear a Boston stairwell](/blog/furniture-wont-fit-up-boston-stairs) sometimes needs to go out a window instead of down the stairs. That's a real, common solution here, not a last resort — but it needs to be identified and priced before moving day, not discovered halfway up a landing with a $6,000 piece wedged sideways.

Measure the stairwell, the landing turns, and any hallway doorframes before you assume a piece will make the trip the conventional way. It's a five-minute check that saves a very bad afternoon.

## Do You Need a Specialty Art Mover, or Will a General Crew Do?

Depends on the scope. A handful of valuable pieces within a normal household move doesn't usually justify a dedicated fine art shipper — a general moving company that offers custom crating, careful handling, and real Full Value Protection is enough for most Boston moves, including a straightforward [local move](/local-moving) across town.

A genuine collection, museum-quality pieces, or a cross-country shipment where the truck sits on the road for days is a different call. That's where a dedicated art logistics company with climate-controlled trucks and dedicated crating shops earns its higher price tag. Ask directly what a company's crew has actually handled before — a mover that's comfortable with a piano is not automatically equipped for a 200-year-old secretary desk with veneer inlay.
