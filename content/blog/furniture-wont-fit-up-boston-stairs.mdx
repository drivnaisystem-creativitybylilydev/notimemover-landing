Measure before you buy, not after the truck shows up. If a couch won't clear a Boston stairwell, hoisting it through a window is the standard fix, and it happens more often here than almost anywhere else in the country.

## How to Actually Measure Before You Move

Most people measure a doorway's width and stop there. That's not enough. What matters is the diagonal depth of the piece itself, the imaginary line running from the top back corner to the bottom front corner, since movers tilt large furniture through doorways at an angle rather than carrying it straight in.

The math is simple: if the diagonal depth is smaller than the width of the narrowest point on the route, the piece can usually be pivoted through. [Furniture-fit guides](https://evefurn.com/blogs/article/how-do-movers-fit-a-sofa-through-a-door-5-expert-techniques-measurement-rules) generally recommend leaving 1-2 inches of clearance beyond that number, not cutting it exactly to size. Measure the sofa's width, depth, height, and diagonal depth, then walk the actual path, front door, hallway turns, interior doors, stairwell landings, and elevator if there is one, and write down the tightest point you find. That number, not the doorway you're staring at, is what decides whether the piece makes it inside.

Removing legs, cushions, and sometimes arm sections shrinks both the size and the handling difficulty. It won't fix a bad route by itself, but it regularly turns a borderline fit into a workable one.

## Why Boston Stairwells Are Different

A lot of Boston's housing stock predates the elevator entirely. Beacon Hill and Back Bay brownstones were built in the 1800s with narrow, winding staircases meant for people, not furniture, and the buildings that do have an elevator often installed a small one decades later as a retrofit, not a full-size freight car. Triple-deckers across Somerville, Allston, and Jamaica Plain have their own version of the same problem: steep, straight-run staircases with a sharp turn at the landing that a king mattress or a sectional simply can't make.

None of this shows up until move day if nobody measured first. A couch that moved fine out of a big-box furniture store's loading dock can be completely unworkable on a third-floor walk-up with a 90-degree turn four steps from the top.

South End bow-fronts add their own wrinkle: many were subdivided into condos decades after they were built, so the "main" staircase splits partway up into a narrower service stair for the upper units. A sofa that clears the ground-floor entry can still stall out one flight up, on a run of stairs half the width of the one below it. It's worth asking a seller or landlord which staircase actually serves your unit before you assume the wider one does.

## What Are Your Options When Something Won't Fit?

There's a real order of operations here, and hoisting isn't the first move.

Start with disassembly. Bed frames, tabletops, and some sectionals come apart with a hex key faster than most people expect, and a piece in three or four smaller sections almost always solves the problem on its own. From there, door removal is next: pulling an interior door off its hinges can open up another inch or two, which is sometimes exactly what's missing. A stairwell banister is occasionally removable too, though that's a job for the crew, not something to loosen yourself the morning of the move.

If disassembly and door removal don't get you there, hoisting is the next real option, not a sign that the move went wrong. It's a normal part of moving in a city with this much pre-war housing stock, and plenty of Boston crews handle it routinely. The order matters because each step is cheaper and faster than the one after it. A ten-minute job with a hex key beats a scheduled crane by a wide margin, so it's worth ruling out the simple fixes before assuming a piece needs to go out a window.

## What Is Furniture Hoisting, Exactly?

Hoisting means moving a piece through a window or balcony opening from the outside, instead of forcing it through an interior stairwell it was never going to fit. There are two versions of it.

Manual hoisting uses a rope-and-pulley rig, usually for a second or third-floor window, and it's something a lot of local moving crews can set up themselves with the right straps and enough hands. Crane or boom-lift hoisting is the heavier-duty version, used for higher floors or pieces too big or heavy for a manual rig, and it typically means bringing in equipment and an operator who does this specifically.

Either way, the item gets wrapped, secured with proper rigging, and lifted or lowered through the opening while the street below is kept clear. It looks dramatic if you've never seen it before. For the crew doing it, it's routine.

## What Does Hoisting Cost in Boston?

Pricing depends heavily on the method, the floor, and what the street allows for setup.

| Method | Typical Floor | Cost Per Item |
|---|---|---|
| Manual rope-and-pulley hoist | 2nd-3rd floor | $100-$300 |
| Crane or boom-lift hoist | 4th floor and up, or heavy items | $300-$1,500+ |
| Piano or safe hoist (crane) | Any upper floor | $500-$1,500+ |

[Cost data on furniture moving](https://www.homeadvisor.com/cost/storage-and-organization/move-furniture/) backs up that range, and the variation comes down to setup time and equipment, not the actual lift itself. A crane needs street access to park and stage, which is its own negotiation on a narrow North End block. A manual hoist just needs a window that opens wide enough and a clear patch of sidewalk below.

Ask about this when you get your quote, not the week of your move. Hoisting almost always costs more as a same-week add-on than as something priced in from the start, mostly because crane rental and rigging specialists book out in advance the same way movers do.

## Which Neighborhoods See This the Most?

Beacon Hill, Back Bay, and the South End top the list, largely because of the [narrow stairwells and tight layouts](/blog/hardest-boston-neighborhoods-to-move) that come with 19th-century construction. The North End has similar issues, plus streets too narrow in places for a full-size truck, let alone a crane. Somerville, Allston, and JP see it too, mostly with triple-deckers where a third-floor unit has a straight, steep staircase with no room to pivot a large piece at the top landing.

If you're apartment hunting in any of these areas and already own a sectional, a king bed frame, or anything oversized, it's worth asking about stairwell width before you sign a lease, not after the moving truck is scheduled. Newer construction in the Seaport and parts of Dorchester rarely has this problem at all, since those buildings were designed around full-size elevators and modern fire code from the start. The issue tracks almost entirely with a building's age, not its neighborhood's reputation or price point.

## How to Avoid Needing a Hoist in the First Place

The cheapest fix is measuring before you buy furniture, not after it's delivered. A modular sectional that breaks into individual seats, a sofa with removable legs and arms, or a bed frame that folds flat all sidestep the entire problem without any extra cost or equipment. If you're furnishing a new place sight-unseen, ask the building or a past tenant about the stairwell before ordering anything oversized.

If you already own the piece and you're not sure it'll make the trip, [taking it apart yourself ahead of time](/blog/furniture-disassembly-boston-move) is worth trying before assuming a hoist is inevitable. And if the building has a freight elevator instead of stairs, that opens up its own set of [reservation and insurance requirements](/blog/boston-high-rise-moving-elevator-coi) worth sorting out early, since a freight elevator booked in advance is usually cheaper and simpler than a hoist arranged at the last minute.

## Booking a Hoist: What to Ask Your Movers

Get specific before you book. Ask whether the crew does manual hoisting themselves or subcontracts it, what it costs per item, and whether they need a permit or a sidewalk closure for the equipment. Boston requires its own paperwork for a lot of curb-related work, similar to the [parking permit process for a moving truck](/blog/boston-moving-truck-parking-permit), so a crane blocking part of a street is not something to sort out same-day.

Crews that do a lot of Beacon Hill and Back Bay work usually flag a likely hoist during the [in-home or virtual estimate](/local-moving), before it becomes a move-day surprise. If your mover hasn't asked about stairwell width or a specific problem piece, bring it up yourself. It's a five-minute conversation that avoids a stalled move and an unplanned few hundred dollars at the door.
