Start with the mattress and the closet, not the nightstand. Those two take the longest and decide how many boxes you actually need for the rest of the room.

## Skip the Boxes You Don't Need First

Before a single box gets taped shut, walk the room and pull out what isn't coming with you. Off-season clothes you haven't touched in a year, the drawer of old chargers, the shoes that don't fit: none of that needs to survive a move just because it's always been in that dresser.

This isn't just decluttering advice for its own sake. Every item you cut is a box you don't pack, don't carry down a flight of stairs, and don't unpack in the new place. [Our full decluttering guide](/blog/declutter-before-a-boston-move) goes deeper on what to donate versus toss, but for a bedroom specifically, the closet and the bottom dresser drawer are where the most forgotten volume hides.

Once that's done, everyday clothing on hangers can move straight into a wardrobe box rather than getting folded into a suitcase. If you're not sure how many you'll need, [our closet-packing guide](/blog/how-to-pack-a-closet-boston-move) has the exact math based on rod length.

## How Many Boxes Does a Boston Bedroom Actually Take?

A primary bedroom in a typical Boston apartment runs 8-12 boxes once the closet is handled separately. That number climbs with a walk-in closet or a lot of décor, and drops for a smaller guest room.

| Category | Boxes Needed | Box Size |
|---|---|---|
| Dresser and nightstand contents | 2-3 | Small-medium |
| Linens, bedding, pillows | 1-2 | Large |
| Décor, lamps, frames | 2-3 | Small-medium |
| Closet (beyond wardrobe boxes) | 2-3 | Medium |
| Kid's or guest room (whole room) | 5-8 | Mixed |

Weight matters more than most people expect once boxes hit a Boston stairwell. Books, and anything else dense, belong in small boxes kept under about 40 pounds. Bedding and other light, bulky items can go in large boxes, which [Public Storage's box-size guide](https://www.publicstorage.com/blog/moving/moving-guide-what-size-boxes-do-you-need.html) notes should still stay under roughly 50 pounds even though they look like they can hold more. A large box stuffed with hardcovers isn't a packing shortcut, it's a box nobody on the crew wants to pick up.

## The Mattress Rule Most People Skip

A bare mattress gets dragged down a hallway, leaned against a truck wall, and set on a dolly, and none of that is kind to it. A sealed mattress bag is the one item in this room that isn't optional.

[U-Haul's guide to choosing a mattress bag](https://www.uhaul.com/Tips/Packing/Choosing-The-Best-Mattress-Bag-for-Moving-And-Storage-22783/) puts it plainly: thinner 1-2 mil bags are fine for a short, dry, local move, but 3 mil is the safer standard for anything with stairs, distance, or real weather involved. Boston weather rarely cooperates for long, so the thicker bag is worth the extra few dollars.

There's a second reason to bag it besides moisture and scuffs. If you've had any run-in with bed bugs at the old place, an unsealed mattress is exactly how they travel to the new one. Our [bed bug prevention guide](/blog/bed-bugs-boston-move) covers how to check before you pack, and a sealed bag is cheap insurance either way. If the mattress itself is on its way out instead of coming with you, our [mattress disposal guide](/blog/mattress-disposal-boston-move) covers how Boston actually handles that, since you can't just leave it on the curb.

## Break Down the Bed Frame Before Moving Day

A basic metal frame usually survives intact. Anything else, platform beds, sleigh beds, a headboard bolted to the rails, almost always needs to come apart first.

This is where a lot of Boston bedrooms get stuck. Triple-decker stairwells and narrow hallway turns in Somerville, JP, and Allston don't leave much room for a queen headboard to pivot around a landing, and a frame that won't clear the turn assembled has to come apart anyway, just later and under more pressure. Doing it the night before, with the hardware bagged and taped to a rail, saves that decision for a moment when nobody's holding up a stairwell.

If you're not confident taking a bed frame apart yourself, our [furniture disassembly guide](/blog/furniture-disassembly-boston-move) walks through the common frame types, and our guide on [furniture that won't fit up Boston stairs](/blog/furniture-wont-fit-up-boston-stairs) covers what to do when a piece genuinely won't clear no matter what comes apart.

## Do Dresser Drawers Actually Need to Be Emptied?

Not entirely, and this is one of the few real time-savers in this room. Lightweight folded clothing can stay right where it is if the drawers come out of the frame and get wrapped separately, whether in stretch wrap or a moving blanket. That's fewer boxes to pack, fewer to unpack, and less to carry as separate pieces.

Heavy items are the exception. Books, a stack of belts with metal buckles, or a jewelry box add real weight fast, and a drawer bottom isn't built to carry that plus its own contents through a truck ride. Pull those out into a small box instead, and keep the dresser itself light enough for two people to carry through a doorway without the drawers sliding out mid-turn.

## Nightstands, Lamps, and the Boxes Everyone Forgets

Nightstand drawers empty fast since there's rarely much in them, but the contents scatter easily if they go loose into a general box. A single small box per nightstand, labeled clearly, keeps chargers, glasses, and whatever else lives in that drawer together instead of turning up in three different rooms later.

Lamps are the other item that gets an afterthought treatment and then arrives broken. Remove the shade and bulb, wrap the base separately, and box the shade on its own rather than nesting it inside anything else. It's a five-minute job that prevents the most common lamp casualty of a move: a crushed shade from something set on top of it in the truck.

## What About the Mirror Over the Dresser?

Anything bigger than a standard picture frame — a full-length mirror, dresser-top mirror, or large framed art — needs a flat mirror or picture box, not just a blanket. These are cardboard sleeves built specifically for flat, fragile items, adjustable to size, and they run a few dollars more than a standard box at any moving supply store.

Skip the box and wrap it in a blanket alone, and you're betting that nobody bumps a doorframe or sets something heavier on top of it in transit. For a piece that can't be replaced with a quick Amazon order, that's not a bet worth making to save four dollars.

## Packing a Kid's Bedroom Is a Different Job

A kid's room usually has less valuable furniture than a primary bedroom, but more small, loose stuff that doesn't sort itself into a standard box. Toys, art supplies, loose puzzle pieces, and stuffed animals move better in clear bins than in taped boxes, since a bin doesn't need to be fully unpacked just to find one specific toy on the first night in the new place.

Pack a kid's room last and unpack it first. Keeping their space functional through as much of the move as possible, and getting it set back up before anything else, makes the whole transition easier on a kid who didn't have a say in any of it. Our [guide to moving with kids in Boston](/blog/moving-with-kids-boston) covers the rest of that side of the process.

On the jobs our crews run across Somerville, JP, and the triple-deckers of Dorchester, the bedroom is usually where the stairwell makes the call: an assembled bed frame either clears the turn or it doesn't, and there's no in-between. That's part of why we quote the job with the building in mind up front instead of after the crew's already stuck on a landing.
