Half the furniture that gets damaged on Boston moves never had to be. It got walked down a stairwell assembled when ten minutes with a screwdriver would have gotten it through clean.

## What to Disassemble Before Movers Arrive

Start with anything that won't clear a doorway, stairwell landing, or tight turn at full size. Bed frames are the obvious one: headboard, footboard, and rails almost always separate with a few bolts, and most take 15-30 minutes solo. Large dressers over five feet wide should lose their legs if they have them. Modular sectionals should split back into individual sections rather than getting wrestled through a hallway as one piece.

Dining tables with removable legs or leaves are worth taking apart even when they'd technically fit, since a bare tabletop is far easier to angle around a corner than a full table with legs catching on the banister. Bookshelves and wardrobes over six feet should lose their shelves and doors at minimum.

Anything you're unsure about, measure first. Measure the furniture at its widest and tallest, then measure every doorway, stairwell width, landing depth, and hallway turn it needs to pass through. If the numbers are close, disassemble. Guessing costs more than ten minutes with an Allen wrench.

## What Not to Disassemble Yourself

Pianos, gun safes, pool tables, and anything with hydraulics or a glass top belong to specialists, not a Saturday-morning YouTube tutorial. These items have internal tensioning, precise alignment, or enough weight that a mistake means real damage or a real injury. [Pianos, safes, and pool tables](/blog/moving-pianos-safes-pool-tables-boston) need dedicated equipment and technique that a standard moving crew, let alone a DIY attempt, isn't set up to handle.

The same goes for anything load-bearing in a way that isn't obvious, like a bed frame with a center support beam that's also holding the mattress foundation, or a sectional with a hidden metal frame connecting the sections. If a piece resists after the visible screws are out, stop. There's usually a hidden fastener you haven't found, not a reason to force it.

## The Boston Stairwell Problem

This is where disassembly stops being optional. Triple-deckers and walk-ups without an elevator are the default in Somerville, Allston, Jamaica Plain, and large parts of Cambridge and Southie, and their stairwells were built before anyone owned a queen mattress or a sectional sofa. Narrow treads, tight 90-degree turns at the landing, and low ceiling clearance on the way up are standard, not the exception.

A couch that clears a modern doorway in the suburbs can get stuck cold on a triple-decker's second-floor turn. [Some of Boston's older neighborhoods](/blog/hardest-boston-neighborhoods-to-move) make this worse with narrow front hallways on top of the stairwell itself, so the tight spot isn't always where you'd expect it. Crews working these buildings regularly will pull cushions, remove legs, and stand a couch on end before they'll try to force it around a corner, because forcing it is how walls, banisters, and furniture corners get damaged.

If your building has a tight stairwell and you're not sure whether a piece will make the turn, assume it won't and disassemble it partway, at least enough to shave a few inches off the widest dimension.

## Tools You'll Actually Need

Most furniture disassembly for a Boston apartment move needs less than you'd think. Here's the realistic list:

| Tool | What It's For |
|---|---|
| Phillips and flathead screwdrivers | Most furniture screws, cam lock covers |
| Allen wrench set | Bed frames, flat-pack furniture, some tables |
| Pliers | Gripping stripped screws, holding nuts in place |
| Rubber mallet | Loosening stuck dowels or joints without marring the wood |
| Cordless drill with driver bits | Speeds up anything with more than a handful of screws |
| Sandwich bags and painter's tape | Hardware organization |

[U-Haul's own guide to furniture assembly and disassembly](https://www.uhaul.com/Tips/Moving-Help/Furniture-Assembly-And-Disassembly-What-Movers-Can-Do-for-You-35853/) recommends having every tool staged before you start on the first piece, rather than hunting for an Allen key halfway through a bed frame with the mattress already off and the room half-packed.

## How to Keep Track of Every Screw

This is the step people skip and regret. As soon as a screw, bolt, or cam lock comes out, it goes into a labeled bag, and that bag gets taped directly to a flat, visible surface on the same piece of furniture. Not a junk drawer, not a pocket, not a shared bag with hardware from three other items.

[Angi's step-by-step disassembly guide](https://www.angi.com/articles/how-disassemble-furniture-move.htm) recommends photographing each piece before you start, particularly close-ups of how panels and hardware connect, so reassembly on the other end doesn't turn into guesswork. That thirty seconds of photos on move-out day saves an hour of frustration on move-in day.

Keep instruction manuals with the disassembled piece if you still have them. If you don't, most furniture brands, IKEA included, have their assembly instructions posted online under the model number.

## IKEA and Flat-Pack Furniture: Special Rules

Flat-pack furniture is built for one assembly, not repeated moves. The particleboard core and cam-lock hardware that make it cheap and easy to build the first time don't hold up to being carried fully assembled down a flight of stairs. Joints loosen, panels crack at the cam lock holes, and doors that were flush when built come out of alignment.

Take it apart fully rather than partially. Start from the top of the piece and work down, removing doors, drawers, and shelves before touching the frame itself. Bag hardware by section, not by furniture piece, if the item has more than one distinct assembly (a wardrobe with a separate hanging rail unit, for example). And don't expect a heavily reused flat-pack piece, one that's already been assembled and disassembled once or twice before, to survive a third round in great shape. Sometimes replacing it is genuinely cheaper than the cam locks it would take to keep it standing.

## Who's Liable If Something Breaks?

Movers are generally responsible for damage that happens while your belongings are in their custody, being carried, loaded, or transported. Disassembly you did yourself before they arrived is typically outside that coverage, since it happened before the item was ever in their hands. If a dresser you took apart wasn't reassembled correctly and a drawer front falls off in transit, that's a different conversation than a drawer front the crew cracked against a doorframe.

This is one more reason to disassemble carefully and bag hardware properly. It's also worth understanding [what your mover's insurance actually covers](/blog/moving-insurance-boston) before move day, since coverage details vary and "fully insured" doesn't mean every scenario is treated the same way.

## Should You Just Let the Movers Handle It?

For anything you're not confident about, yes. A reasonable local crew will disassemble standard furniture as part of the job, and it's worth asking what's included before you spend a Saturday morning with an Allen wrench on something they'd handle in ten minutes anyway.
