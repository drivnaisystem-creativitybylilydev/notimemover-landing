Short version: inspect every seam on secondhand furniture before it comes inside, wrap mattresses in plastic instead of moving blankets, and never skip that step just because move day is already chaotic.

## Why Moving Is Prime Time for Bed Bugs

Most bed bug problems don't start with pests choosing your home. They start with something carrying pests choosing your home, and moving day is the single riskiest day for that to happen. You're handling secondhand furniture, digging through storage, and packing everything you own into cardboard boxes that sit in a truck with dozens of other households' belongings.

Bed bugs are built for exactly this. They can survive for months without a meal, they flatten thin enough to hide in a mattress seam or a book spine, and they don't announce themselves the way a roach or a mouse does. A box that looks perfectly clean when you tape it shut can still be carrying them when you open it three weeks later in a new apartment.

None of that means you should panic. It means the few minutes you spend inspecting before you pack are worth more than almost anything else on a moving checklist.

## Is Boston Really a Bed Bug City?

Boston isn't at the top of the national bed bug rankings. Orkin's 2026 Top 50 Bed Bug Cities list, based on treatment volume, puts Boston in the bottom half of the list, well behind cities like Chicago and Los Angeles. So this isn't a five-alarm crisis.

It's also not nothing. Boston's housing stock skews toward dense multi-family buildings, triple-deckers with shared walls, and neighborhoods with heavy student and short-term rental turnover, all of which make it easier for an infestation in one unit to become a problem for the building next door. Add a moving culture built around a single lease-turnover date, and you get a lot of secondhand furniture changing hands on the same few days every year.

That combination is why this is worth ten minutes of attention even in a city that isn't a national hotspot.

## Inspect What You Already Own Before You Pack

Start with your own home before you worry about anyone else's furniture. The [EPA's guidance on finding bed bugs](https://www.epa.gov/bedbugs/how-find-bed-bugs) is specific for a reason: bites are a bad indicator on their own, since they can look like mosquito bites, hives, or a mild allergic reaction. What actually confirms an infestation is physical evidence.

Check mattress and box spring seams, the piping along cushions, and the underside of furniture with a flashlight. Look for:

- Live bugs, usually reddish-brown and about the size of an apple seed
- Rust-colored or dark spots along seams, which are droppings
- Pale, shed exoskeletons in folds and cracks

Pay extra attention to anything that's been in a basement, a storage unit, or a dorm room, since those are the pieces most likely to have picked something up without your knowledge.

If everything's clean, you're not done. This is also your moment to [declutter before the actual pack begins](/blog/declutter-before-a-boston-move) — fewer items in transit means fewer hiding spots, full stop.

## The Curb-Alert Rule: Should You Ever Take Free Furniture?

Boston has a real curb-alert culture, especially in the weeks around [September 1](/blog/moving-september-1-boston), when a huge share of the city's leases turn over on the same day and sidewalks fill with dressers, mattresses, and couches nobody wanted to pay to move. It's tempting. It's also exactly when this goes wrong most often.

A mattress or upholstered piece sitting on a curb has an unknown history and no way to prove otherwise. If you're going to take something free, the rule is simple: inspect every seam and joint in daylight, and if you can't get a clear look at all of it, or you spot anything questionable, walk away. This applies even more to mattresses and box springs than to hard furniture like dressers or bookshelves, since fabric and padding are where bed bugs actually live.

If you're the one setting furniture out instead of picking it up, treat it the same way in reverse. If there's any doubt, don't just leave it curbside for the next person; check [Boston's mattress disposal rules](/blog/mattress-disposal-boston-move) instead.

## How to Pack So Bed Bugs Can't Hitch a Ride

Even with a clean home, packing method matters. Cardboard is porous and reusable, which makes it a worse barrier than most people assume. Plastic storage bins with sealing lids are a better choice for anything soft, like linens, pillows, and stuffed animals, precisely because there's nothing for a bug to burrow into.

For mattresses and box springs, use a sealed plastic mattress encasement rather than just a moving blanket. Blankets get reused from job to job across dozens of households, so wrapping a mattress in plastic instead removes that transfer risk entirely rather than trusting that the last job's equipment was clean.

Wash and dry anything fabric on the hottest safe setting before you pack it, since heat is one of the few things that reliably kills bed bugs and their eggs. Pack clean laundry in clear bags so it's obvious at a glance what's already been treated and what hasn't.

## What Should You Ask a Moving Company Before You Book?

This is the part most bed bug guides skip entirely, and it matters. A mover's blankets, dollies, and truck interiors touch dozens of homes a month. A reputable company treats and inspects that equipment on a regular schedule; a fly-by-night outfit running a shared truck between random jobs may not think about it at all.

Ask directly when you're [vetting a moving company](/blog/how-to-choose-a-moving-company-boston): how often is equipment cleaned, and what happens if a crew flags a concern on a previous job. A company with nothing to hide will answer plainly. One that gets cagey about a simple pest question is telling you something about how it runs the rest of its operation, too.

Crews working [local Boston moves](/local-moving) day after day see a wide range of secondhand furniture come off triple-decker porches and out of storage units, so this isn't a hypothetical concern for the industry. It's a routine one, which is exactly why it should be a routine question.

## Checking Your New Place Before the Truck Arrives

Don't wait until move-in day to look at your new unit. If you can get access even a day or two early, walk through with a flashlight and check along baseboards, behind outlet covers, and around any built-in furniture the previous tenant left behind.

This matters more in multi-family buildings, where an infestation in a neighboring unit can spread through shared walls or a shared laundry room, and pinning down whether a problem started before or after move-in gets a lot harder once your own boxes are already inside. If you spot anything concerning, raise it with the landlord in writing before your lease start date, not after.

A clean walkthrough costs you almost nothing. A problem discovered after your furniture is already unpacked costs a lot more, in both money and time.

## What Does Bed Bug Treatment Actually Cost?

If you do find something, cost and timeline both depend heavily on which treatment method you use. Here's the general range, per [Angi's 2026 cost data](https://www.angi.com/articles/how-much-does-bed-bug-extermination-cost.htm):

| Treatment Type | Typical Cost | Visits Needed |
|---|---|---|
| Chemical treatment, per room | $270-$775 | 2-3 visits, spaced 10-14 days apart |
| Whole-unit heat treatment | $1-$3 per sq ft | Usually 1 visit, 6-8 hours |
| Full apartment (avg. 1BR) | $1,000-$2,500 | Varies by method |

Heat treatment costs more upfront but clears an infestation in a single session, which is often worth it when you're working around a fixed move date. Chemical treatment is cheaper per visit but stretches over several weeks, which doesn't line up well with a lease that's already ending.

Either way, don't move anything out of an affected room until a professional has cleared it. Moving boxes to a storage unit or a new apartment before treatment is how a contained problem turns into two separate ones.
