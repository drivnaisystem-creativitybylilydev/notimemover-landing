The kitchen is the heaviest room in your apartment to pack, pound for pound, and the one most people save for last. Here's how to do it without cracking a plate or blowing out a box on the stairs.

## Start With the Pantry, Not the Cabinets

Most people open with dishes because that's what feels urgent. Start with the pantry instead, since it's the easiest decision to make and it's the room where a hard call saves you real money.

Go through everything and sort into three piles: eat it before moving day, donate it, or box it. Anything unopened and non-perishable can go to a neighborhood food pantry — Boston has dozens, many of them partner agencies of the [Greater Boston Food Bank](https://www.gbfb.org/), and most accept sealed, unexpired donations right up until moving week. Canned goods and glass jars are dense and heavy, and if your quote is priced by weight for a long-distance move, a stocked pantry adds real cost to move a can of beans you'd spend $1.50 replacing at the new place.

For local moves priced hourly, weight matters less on the bill but still matters on the stairs. Either way, a lighter pantry is a lighter kitchen.

## How Many Boxes Will Your Kitchen Actually Take?

Kitchens punch above their size. A studio kitchen with a modest dish set still needs more boxes than a bedroom twice its square footage, because everything in it is dense.

| Household Size | Kitchen Boxes | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Studio / 1BR | 8-12 | Basic dishware, few small appliances |
| 2BR, light cook | 12-16 | Full dish set, 2-3 small appliances |
| 2BR+, heavy cook | 15-20 | Bakeware, gadgets, stocked pantry |

Small boxes for dishes, canned goods, and cookbooks. Medium boxes for pots, pans, and small appliances. Skip large boxes in the kitchen almost entirely — they look efficient but end up either half-empty or dangerously heavy once you fill them with anything ceramic or glass. If you're still sourcing boxes, [liquor store boxes](/blog/free-moving-boxes-boston) are worth grabbing specifically for this room. They're built for glass bottles, so the cardboard is thicker than a standard moving box, and a lot of them come with built-in dividers that work well for glassware.

## Packing Plates, Glasses, and Everyday Dishes

The technique itself doesn't change room to room: wrap every fragile item individually, load plates on their edge like records in a crate rather than stacked flat, and stuff glasses with paper before wrapping the outside. The full method is in our [fragile items packing guide](/blog/packing-fragile-items-boston-move) if you want the step-by-step.

What's specific to the kitchen is volume. You're not wrapping one vase, you're wrapping forty pieces of dishware in an afternoon, and the temptation is to speed up by skipping the paper stuffing on "just the everyday plates" since they're not the good china. Don't. Everyday dishes break just as easily as the good set, and they're the ones you'll actually miss when they're gone.

[U-Haul's kitchen packing guide](https://www.uhaul.com/Tips/Packing/How-To-Pack-A-Kitchen-for-Moving-18802/) recommends dedicated dish-pack kits with foam pouches and cell dividers for anything glass or ceramic, which is worth the small extra cost over generic boxes if you're packing a full set. [PODS' dish-packing guide](https://www.pods.com/blog/how-to-pack-dishes-for-moving) makes the same point about never nesting cups directly inside each other without a pouch or paper between them — even a soft-seeming stack can chip at the rim.

## Pots, Pans, and Small Appliances

Pots and pans nest, which makes them deceptively fast to pack and deceptively heavy once nested. Stack two or three per box with a sheet of packing paper between each, and stop there rather than filling the box to the top.

Small appliances — coffee maker, toaster, blender, stand mixer — travel best in their original boxes if you kept them. If not, wrap each one in paper or a towel, tape down any loose cords or parts, and pack them upright in a medium box with padding on all sides. A stand mixer alone can justify its own box; it's heavy enough to throw off the balance of anything packed around it. Full-service crews will usually wrap these for you if you ask, but on an hourly labor-only job, don't assume it's included — confirm with your crew ahead of time.

Knives get their own short list: wrap each blade separately, point-in, and mark the box "sharp" in addition to whatever else you label it. A knife block wrapped as a unit is a common source of cut hands on move day.

## Large Appliances Are a Different Job Entirely

Your fridge, stove, and dishwasher aren't kitchen-packing tasks — they're a separate category with their own prep, and they're not something to handle the same afternoon you're wrapping glasses. A fridge that's still cold and full when the crew arrives is the single most common kitchen delay on move day, and it's an easy one to avoid with a little lead time.

Start emptying and defrosting the fridge at least 24 hours before your movers arrive, not the morning of. A gas range or dryer needs a licensed plumber to disconnect it in Massachusetts, not your moving crew, so that call needs to happen days ahead, not the night before.

If a fridge, washer, or gas range is coming with you, give it its own prep window and read our [appliance moving guide](/blog/moving-appliances-boston) for the full timeline, dolly requirements, and what Massachusetts law requires for gas disconnection. Treat it as a separate checklist item from the rest of the kitchen, not something you'll get to "after the boxes."

## Why Box Weight Matters More Here Than Anywhere Else in the Apartment

Kitchen items are the densest thing you own. A box of hardcover books and a box of dinner plates can look the same size and weigh completely differently, and it's the kitchen boxes that most often cross the line movers actually care about. Most moving companies ask for boxes under 50 lbs, and dishes hit that ceiling fast even in a box that still looks half-full.

That matters more in a Boston walk-up than it does anywhere with an elevator. A lot of triple-decker kitchens sit at the back of the unit, down a narrow galley run to a stairwell that wasn't built with movers in mind. We see this pattern on plenty of Boston jobs: an overpacked 50-lb dish box turns what should be a quick one-person carry into a slow two-person shuffle down a landing built for foot traffic, not freight. Kitchen boxes packed to 35-40 lbs instead of maxed out move faster and survive the trip better, on the stairs specifically.

It's also one of the few places where a smaller box genuinely beats a bigger one. Buy more small boxes than you think you need for this room. You'll use every one of them.

## What Goes in Your "Open First" Box?

Pack one box last, load it onto the truck last, and label it in big letters so nobody buries it under everything else. This is the box that saves you from eating takeout standing over an unopened dish box on night one.

Put in it: one pot, one pan, a handful of plates and utensils for two people, a coffee maker or kettle, dish soap and a sponge, a dish towel, and a roll of paper towels. That's enough to make coffee and cook a simple meal before the rest of the kitchen gets unpacked, which usually happens over the following few days rather than all at once.

If you're moving locally within Boston, this is also the box worth handing directly to your crew lead with instructions to load it last. On a [local move](/local-moving), that's a five-second conversation that saves a real headache later.
