Splitting a move three or four ways sounds simple until the bill arrives and nobody agrees on what "fair" means. Here's how to actually divide it.

## Why Splitting a Move Isn't as Simple as Dividing by Three

The instinct is to take the total bill and divide by the number of roommates. That works fine when everyone has roughly the same amount of stuff and the same size room. It falls apart the moment one person has a queen bed, a dresser, and six boxes while another has a mattress on the floor and two duffel bags.

Money isn't the only tangle, either. Boston leases hold every named tenant responsible for the whole unit, a detail that matters more at move-out than most roommates realize until it's an issue. And roommate groups almost never move out on the exact same day, which complicates everything from the [security deposit](/blog/get-security-deposit-back-boston) to who's still around to help carry the couch.

None of this is a reason to avoid moving with roommates. It's just a reason to settle the money and logistics questions before move day, not during it.

## How to Actually Split the Moving Bill

There are three common methods, and none of them is universally "correct." Pick the one that matches your situation and agree on it in writing before you book a mover.

| Method | How It Works | Best For |
|---|---|---|
| Equal split | Total cost divided evenly, regardless of room size or amount of stuff | Roommates with similar-size rooms and similar belongings |
| Room-size weighted | Whoever has the bigger room or more furniture pays a larger share | Mismatched bedroom sizes or one roommate bringing most of the shared furniture |
| Rent-percentage weighted | Split mirrors what each person already pays in rent | Groups where rent is already unequal for a reason (bigger room, private bath) |

Whichever method you pick, write down the dollar amount each person owes before the truck shows up, not after. A [local Boston move](/local-moving) for a shared 2-3 bedroom apartment commonly runs $600-$1,400 depending on floor and stairs, and that's a number worth agreeing on in advance rather than fighting over on move day.

For anything beyond the mover's bill, like boxes, tape, or a pizza for whoever helps carry, apps like Splitwise handle the running tally better than a group chat full of Venmo requests nobody remembers to settle.

## What Is Joint and Several Liability, and Why Does It Matter Here?

This is the part most roommate groups never look into until something goes wrong. In Massachusetts, when multiple people sign the same lease, the landlord treats them as jointly and severally liable, per [Nolo's tenant-rights guide](https://www.nolo.com/landlord-tenant/roommate-wants-to-leave-early.html), which means every named tenant is on the hook for the full obligation, not just their proportional share.

In practice: if a roommate skips town before move-out without paying their share of a damage charge, or the unit gets left in bad shape and one person doesn't pitch in for cleaning, the landlord can legally go after any remaining tenant for the whole amount. It doesn't matter whose fault it actually was. This is standard across most U.S. leases, not a Massachusetts quirk, but it's worth knowing cold before you're the one covering someone else's share.

It's also why a verbal "we'll figure it out" agreement isn't enough. If your group is splitting a security deposit or move-out costs, put the split in writing before anyone signs the lease, ideally, and definitely before anyone moves out.

## What Happens to the Security Deposit When Roommates Move Out at Different Times?

This trips up more Boston roommate groups than almost anything else. Under [Massachusetts law](https://www.mass.gov/info-details/mass-general-laws-c186-ss-15b), the security deposit belongs to the tenancy, not to individual tenants. Most landlords hold one deposit and return it as a single check, made out to all original tenants, once the entire unit has been vacated, not when the first person moves out.

That means a roommate who leaves in June for a July sublet elsewhere might not see their share of the deposit until everyone else is out in August or September. If your group has staggered move-out dates, which is common when only some of you are renewing, work this out with a written side agreement: who fronts the departing roommate's share and when, and how the eventual landlord check gets divided once it actually arrives.

Waiting until the deposit shows up to have this conversation is how roommate friendships end. Have it early instead.

## Staggered Move-Out Dates Are the Norm, Not the Exception

Full roommate groups rarely move out in perfect sync. Someone's lease ends a week early, someone else is staying in Boston and just switching apartments, and somebody's headed home for a while before the next place is ready. Plan around that instead of assuming everyone's carrying boxes on the same Saturday. Give a shared move more lead time than [a solo one usually needs](/blog/how-far-in-advance-to-book-movers-boston), since a mover has to work around everyone's schedule instead of just one person's, and the group itself needs time to agree on a split before anyone books anything.

This gets sharper around [September 1](/blog/moving-september-1-boston), Boston's mass lease-turnover date. If your group is renewing as a unit and moving to a new apartment together, you're competing with thousands of other households for the same trucks and crews on the same 24-48 hour window. Book 3-4 weeks out minimum if any part of your move touches that date, earlier if your group can manage it, since a shared move takes longer to lock in than a solo one. If your group is splitting up instead, with some renewing and some leaving, coordinate the exact handoff of keys, cleaning duties, and who's still on-site to let the movers in.

Either way, someone in the group should own the calendar. Shared moves fall apart less often because of bad planning and more often because nobody was actually in charge of the planning.

## One Mover for Everyone, or Book Separately?

If everyone's headed to the same new address, or addresses close enough for one truck route, one shared booking is almost always the better deal. You're paying for one crew, one truck, and one trip instead of three separate minimum-hour bookings, and the per-person cost usually comes out lower even after splitting.

Separate bookings make more sense when destinations or dates genuinely don't overlap, or when the cost-splitting conversation is generating more tension than it's saving in money. There's no rule that says a roommate group has to move as one unit just because they lived as one. Sometimes the cleaner answer is everyone handling their own move and splitting only the shared items.

We see both setups on Boston jobs regularly, and the deciding factor is almost never the money. It's whether the group can actually agree on a plan before the truck shows up. A locked-in quote helps either way, since nobody's arguing over a shifting total on top of everything else.

## Whose Stuff Goes on Which Truck?

Shared furniture is where roommate moves get genuinely confusing. The couch, the kitchen table, the TV stand — somebody has to claim it, and it's worth deciding who before moving day, not while the crew is standing in the doorway waiting for an answer.

A few questions worth settling in advance: who's keeping the shared furniture at the new place, does the person keeping it owe the others anything for their share, and does anything need to go into [storage](/blog/boston-storage-options-during-a-move) instead because the new place is smaller or the timing doesn't line up. None of these are hard conversations, but they're much harder mid-move than they are over a group chat two weeks earlier.

Label boxes and furniture by person, not just by room, if you're using one shared crew. "Kitchen" on a box tells the movers where it goes at the old place. It doesn't tell them whose box it is if the group ends up at two different addresses.

## Write a Roommate Moving Agreement Before You Book Anything

You don't need a lawyer for this, just a shared document everyone actually reads and agrees to. Cover four things at minimum: how the moving bill splits, who keeps which shared items, how the security deposit gets divided once it's returned, and what happens if one person's move-out date shifts.

Put dollar amounts and dates in writing, not vague intentions. "We'll split it fairly" means something different to every person in the group until it's an actual number next to an actual name. Fifteen minutes on a shared doc before you book a mover saves weeks of awkward follow-up texts after.
