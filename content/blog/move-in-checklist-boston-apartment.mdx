Your move-in checklist starts before a single box comes off the truck, not after. Here's the order that actually protects your deposit and your sanity.

## Do This Before the Truck Even Shows Up

Get into the unit empty if your building allows it, even just an hour before movers arrive. Walk every room with your phone out and photograph everything: floors, walls, ceilings, inside closets, appliance interiors, window screens. Wide shots first, then close-ups of anything that looks off. Empty rooms photograph clean; rooms with boxes stacked against the walls don't.

While you're in there, find the essentials before you need them under pressure. Locate the circuit breaker panel, the water shutoff valve, and the gas shutoff if the unit has gas appliances. Note where the thermostat is and whether it's a shared building system or unit-specific. In a lot of Boston triple-deckers, heat is steam or hot water radiator, controlled building-wide or floor-by-floor rather than by a single thermostat in your unit, so it's worth asking your landlord directly rather than guessing.

If you haven't already, measure doorways and stairwell turns against your largest furniture. It's a five-minute check that prevents a genuinely bad afternoon when a couch won't clear a landing.

## Is Your Statement of Condition Actually Legit?

Massachusetts law requires more than a handshake here. Under [M.G.L. c. 186 §15B](https://www.mass.gov/info-details/mass-general-laws-c186-ss-15b), a landlord who collects a security deposit must give you a written, signed Statement of Condition within 10 days of your tenancy starting. It has to list existing damage to the unit, not just say "in good condition" as a blanket line.

Read it against your own photos before you sign anything. If it's missing something you can see, like a cracked windowsill or a stained ceiling tile, note it in writing and send a corrected copy back within 15 days. This single document is the difference between an easy security deposit return later and a drawn-out dispute over damage you didn't cause. We've written up [the full deposit-return process](/blog/get-security-deposit-back-boston) if you want the other half of this story, the one that plays out when you eventually move out.

If your landlord never gives you one, that's not a minor oversight. It can limit their ability to withhold your deposit for damage claims down the road, so don't let it slide just because the rest of move-in day is chaotic.

## What Should You Test Before You Unpack a Single Box?

This is the step people skip because they're exhausted and just want to start unpacking the kitchen. Do it anyway, in this order, before the first box gets opened:

Plug something into every outlet in every room, a phone charger works fine. Run every faucet hot and cold, flush every toilet, and check under every sink for existing leaks or water damage. Turn on the stove burners and oven, run the dishwasher and disposal if you have them, and confirm the fridge is actually cold, not just running. Open and close every window and door, including closets, and check that every lock, deadbolt, and window latch actually engages. Ask your landlord directly how many keys exist and who else holds one, since a lock rekey is a fair ask at move-in and a much harder conversation to have later.

None of this takes more than 20 minutes, and it's the difference between catching a broken outlet on day one, when it's clearly a landlord problem, versus discovering it three weeks in, when it's a lot murkier whose fault it is.

Water pressure and hot water recovery time are worth a real test too, not just a quick tap. Run the shower for a full two minutes and see if the hot water holds. Older Boston buildings sometimes share a single water heater across multiple units, and a shower that goes lukewarm fast on move-in day is a pattern that repeats every morning after, not a one-time fluke.

## Are the Smoke and CO Detectors Actually Working?

Don't take it on faith that they work just because they're mounted on the ceiling. Massachusetts landlords are required to install and maintain working smoke detectors and carbon monoxide alarms in every sleeping area and on every habitable level, under [M.G.L. c. 148 §26F and §26F½](https://www.mass.gov/info-details/smoke-and-carbon-monoxide-alarms). Press the test button on each one. If a unit is silent, dead battery, or missing altogether, tell your landlord in writing that same day, not "eventually."

This matters more in older Boston buildings than newer construction. A lot of triple-decker conversions have detectors that were installed at the last tenant turnover and never touched again. A thirty-second test now beats finding out they're dead at 2 a.m.

Check the manufacture date while you're up there, too. Most smoke detectors have a stamped date on the back and a lifespan of about 10 years, after which the sensor itself degrades even with a fresh battery. If the unit predates your lease by a decade, that's a specific, easy thing to flag rather than a vague "please check the detectors" request.

## The Boston Triple-Decker Move-In Quirks

Older Boston buildings come with move-in details that a generic apartment checklist won't mention. Buzzer and intercom systems in triple-deckers are frequently wired oddly or mislabeled with a previous tenant's name, so test yours and ask your landlord to relabel it if needed. Mailbox keys are easy to forget entirely until your first bill doesn't show up; confirm you have one and that it's for the right box.

Shared basements are common for laundry and extra storage in two- and three-family conversions, and it's worth a quick walkthrough to see what's actually accessible to you versus other units. Parking is its own conversation. If you don't have an assigned spot, check what resident permit you'll need and how long the application takes, since it's rarely instant in Boston.

Trash and recycling day matters more here than it does anywhere with a dumpster. Plenty of triple-deckers put barrels curbside on a fixed weekly schedule, and missing the first pickup means a full week of bags piling up in a hallway or a small kitchen. Ask your landlord directly rather than guessing, since the schedule varies block to block and isn't always posted anywhere obvious.

## What If You Find Damage After You've Already Unpacked?

Email it to your landlord the moment you notice it, with a photo attached and the date clearly visible in your message thread. Don't wait to mention it until move-out, and don't just mention it verbally in the hallway. A dated, written record showing you reported something promptly is worth far more than a clear memory months later when a deposit dispute actually happens.

This is also where your original Statement of Condition earns its keep again. If the damage matches something already listed, you're covered. If it's new and you reported it fast, you're still covered. The gap that actually costs people money is silence.

Keep everything in one place from day one. A single email folder or photo album labeled with your address, started the day you get keys, is far more useful eight months later than trying to reconstruct a timeline from scattered texts and camera roll screenshots you can't quite date.

## The Move-In Day Checklist at a Glance

| Category | Check This |
|---|---|
| Documentation | Photograph every room empty, before unpacking |
| Legal | Confirm you received a Statement of Condition within 10 days |
| Electrical | Test every outlet and light switch |
| Plumbing | Run every faucet, flush toilets, check under sinks |
| Appliances | Test stove, oven, fridge temp, dishwasher, disposal |
| Safety | Press-test every smoke and CO detector |
| Security | Check every door and window lock; confirm who has spare keys |
| Building logistics | Buzzer, mailbox key, laundry access, parking permit |
