Fragile items are where moves actually go wrong. Not the couch, not the dresser, the box of wedding china nobody wrapped individually.

## The One Rule That Prevents Most Breakage

Wrap every fragile item on its own. Not "wrap the plates as a stack," not "these two vases can share a box since they're similar sizes." One item, one wrap, every time.

This sounds excessive until you picture what actually happens inside a moving box on a truck. Boxes shift, get stacked, and absorb small bumps over every flight of stairs and every turn the truck takes. Two items touching inside a box will knock against each other on nearly every one of those bumps, and it only takes one hard knock to crack ceramic or chip a frame corner. [ConsumerAffairs' packing guide](https://www.consumeraffairs.com/movers/how-to-pack-fragile-items-for-moving.html) makes the same point: even two items wrapped together can crack against each other, so the individual-wrap rule isn't optional if you want things to survive.

The mechanics are simple. Use packing paper first for anything with a delicate surface, like china or glassware, then a layer of bubble wrap over that, bubbles facing inward so they don't pop against the item itself. Tape the wrap, never the item. Fill every gap in the box with crumpled paper afterward. An unpadded gap is where the shifting starts.

## How Do You Pack Dishes, Glasses, and Mirrors?

Different fragile categories need different handling, and generic "wrap it in paper" advice undersells a few of them.

Plates go in vertically, like records in a crate, never stacked flat. A stack of flat plates concentrates all the weight on the bottom plate's center, which is exactly how plates crack in transit. Standing them on edge spreads the pressure across the rim instead.

Glasses and stemware get stuffed with paper inside the glass itself before wrapping the outside, since an empty glass is structurally weaker than a packed one. Wine glasses in particular need the stem protected separately, not just the bowl.

Mirrors, framed art, and flat-panel TVs need a flat, rigid box, not a padded soft one. A mirror box or a TV box with corrugated corner protectors is built for this specifically. This is one spot where buying the right box actually matters more than how much padding you use inside it.

| Item Type | Wrap With | Box Type |
|---|---|---|
| Plates, bowls | Packing paper, stacked vertically | Small or dish-pack box |
| Glasses, stemware | Paper stuffed inside, then wrapped | Small box, cell dividers if available |
| Mirrors, framed art | Bubble wrap over cardboard corners | Flat mirror/picture box |
| Flat-panel TVs | Anti-static bubble wrap, original box if kept | Telescoping TV box |
| Lamps, lampshades | Shade wrapped separately from base | Tall box, shade on top |

## What Counts as an "Extraordinary Value" Item?

This is the part most packing guides skip, and it matters more than most people realize. [Federal moving regulations](https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/protect-your-move/how-to/subpartA) define an "article of extraordinary value" as anything worth more than $100 per pound: fine art, antiques, silverware, furs, jewelry, and similar items. If you don't list these separately on your shipping documents, the mover can limit their liability on them regardless of which valuation tier you paid for.

In practice, that means a $3,000 painting weighing four pounds is worth $750 per pound, well past the threshold, and it needs its own line item before move day, not a mention after something goes wrong. This is a documentation step, not a packing technique, but skipping it undoes the value of doing everything else right.

## Should Movers Pack Your Fragile Items, or Should You?

Both approaches work. The deciding factor is what you'd actually do if the item broke.

If something breaking would mean a real financial or emotional loss, professional packing, done by the crew, keeps it under the mover's own valuation coverage and their packing standards. If a mover packs an item and it's damaged, that's generally a stronger claims position than if you packed it yourself and something shifted in transit. Most [local moving](/local-moving) bookings can add packing for specific rooms or item categories instead of the whole apartment, which keeps the cost tied to what actually needs the extra care.

If it's fragile but replaceable, a set of everyday glasses, a lamp you don't love, packing it yourself saves on labor cost. [Professional packing typically runs $50-$100 per packer per hour](https://www.thisoldhouse.com/moving/cost-of-professional-packers), and fragile or specialty items can add an extra hour or two of labor per room. Full-service packing for a typical apartment lands around $300-$1,500 depending on how much of that inventory is genuinely fragile, which adds up fast if you're paying it on everything in the apartment instead of just the pieces that actually warrant it.

A reasonable split for most Boston moves: let the crew handle anything irreplaceable or awkward to wrap yourself, like art, mirrors, and electronics, and pack your own everyday fragile items in the days before.

## Boston's Stairs Are the Real Test, Not the Truck Ride

Most fragile-item damage doesn't happen on the highway. It happens in the stairwell.

The city's housing stock is old. Triple-deckers in Somerville, Dorchester, and JP, and walk-ups across Allston and parts of Cambridge, routinely have no elevator and staircases under 36 inches wide. A box that would ride fine in a moving truck can still take a hard knock getting turned around a tight landing three flights up, especially when a crew is trying to move fast on a hot afternoon during a full day of back-to-back jobs.

This is where the individual-wrap rule earns its keep. A box that gets tilted, bumped against a railing, or briefly set down at an awkward angle on a landing is a normal part of a Boston stairwell move, not a sign of a careless crew. Padding for that reality, rather than for an idealized flat carry to a driveway, is what actually prevents breakage here. If your building has a freight elevator instead, ask about booking it and the [certificate of insurance most Boston high-rises require](/blog/boston-high-rise-moving-elevator-coi) well before move day, since that paperwork holdup is its own separate way fragile boxes end up waiting around in a lobby longer than they should.

We see this pattern on nearly every Boston job that involves stairs: the crews that move fastest and cause the least damage are the ones who aren't guessing at pricing mid-job. When your quote and your budget are locked in before the truck shows up, nobody's rushing a flight of stairs to beat the clock on an hourly rate. That's not a fragile-item packing tip specifically, but it's a real reason rushed jobs and broken items tend to show up together.

## What Belongs in Your Car, Not the Truck

Some fragile and valuable items shouldn't go in a moving box at all, regardless of how well you wrap them.

Jewelry, cash, passports, and irreplaceable documents belong with you, not on the truck, full stop. The same goes for anything genuinely sentimental and one-of-a-kind, like family photos without a digital backup or a small heirloom you couldn't replace at any price. If you'd be devastated losing it, it rides with you.

This overlaps with [decluttering before your move](/blog/declutter-before-a-boston-move) in a useful way: sorting through what's actually fragile and valuable versus what just seems that way is easier once you've already gone through the "keep, donate, toss" pass on everything else. Fewer boxes overall also means fewer chances for something fragile to get buried under heavier items by accident.

## If Something Still Breaks

Even a well-packed move can end with something broken. Movers are handling dozens of boxes across stairs, doorways, and a truck ramp, and an occasional mistake happens even with careful crews.

If it does, photograph the damage before you unpack further or throw anything away, and note it on the delivery paperwork before you sign, not after. Most people sign off on "all items received in good condition" out of habit, without actually inspecting first, and that signature makes disputing damage later considerably harder. From there, file a written claim within your mover's stated window.
