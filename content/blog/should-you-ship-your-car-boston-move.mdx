If your move out of Boston is farther than a tank of gas can comfortably cover, you're not just deciding on a moving company — you're deciding what happens to your car.

## Do You Actually Need to Ship Your Car?

Not every long-distance move needs a car carrier. If you're headed to New York or Washington D.C., the drive is short enough that most people just get behind the wheel and go. Past a certain point, though, driving stops being the free option it looks like on paper.

Industry data from [Move.org](https://www.move.org/should-you-ship-your-car-or-drive-it-when-you-move/) puts the real breakeven around 700-1,000 miles. Below that, driving is usually cheaper once you're only paying for gas and maybe a meal on the road. Above it, the math flips: multiple nights in hotels, meals for however many days the drive takes, and mileage added to a car you're about to need in a new city all start adding up fast. Factor in two lost vacation days you could've spent unpacking instead of driving, and the gap closes even more.

For a Boston move to Florida or the Carolinas, both [common exit points once movers leave Boston](/blog/moving-out-of-state-from-boston), that's a 1,300+ mile drive, often two full days behind the wheel minimum. Shipping starts looking a lot more reasonable once you price out the alternative honestly instead of just looking at the sticker cost of a transport quote. A 215-mile trip to New York is a different story entirely. Most people just drive it themselves, and a car carrier for that distance would be overkill.

## What Shipping a Car From Boston Actually Costs

Car shipping is priced by distance, but not in a straight line. Shorter hauls cost more per mile because the carrier's fixed costs, fuel, driver time, insurance, get spread over fewer miles. Longer routes get cheaper per mile as the trip fills out the trailer's capacity.

| Route From Boston | Approx. Distance | Open Transport Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Boston to New York City | ~215 miles | $400-$700 |
| Boston to Washington, D.C. | ~450 miles | $550-$900 |
| Boston to the Carolinas | ~800-950 miles | $750-$1,100 |
| Boston to Florida | ~1,300-1,500 miles | $900-$1,400 |

These numbers track industry cost guides fairly closely: cross-country hauls in the 2,000-mile range commonly run $1,200-$2,500 on an open carrier, per data compiled by [moveBuddha](https://www.movebuddha.com/blog/drive-car-or-ship-it/). SUVs and trucks usually add another $100-$200 over what a sedan costs, since they take up more trailer space. Get quotes from at least two carriers before booking either way. Prices vary more than people expect for the exact same route and pickup week.

## Open Transport or Enclosed: Which Do You Need?

Open transport is what most people use, and it's what carries [roughly 90% of vehicles shipped in the U.S.](https://www.move.org/open-vs-enclosed-car-shipping/) today. Your car rides on an uncovered multi-car trailer alongside several others, exposed to weather and road spray the same way it would be if you drove it yourself.

Enclosed transport puts your car in a covered trailer, usually carrying just one to four vehicles instead of eight or more. That lower volume per trip is exactly why it costs 30-60% more than open transport. Unless you're shipping a classic car, something with real collector value, or a brand-new vehicle you don't want picking up a single highway chip, open transport is the right call for almost every household move.

## When Driving Still Wins

Shipping isn't automatically the smarter move just because the distance is long. If you're already planning to caravan with a rental truck, need the car at your destination the same week you leave, or genuinely don't mind the miles, driving keeps things simpler. One less company to coordinate, one less handoff where something could go wrong.

Winter is where this calculus changes fastest. A February drive down I-95 after a Nor'easter is a different trip than the same route in June, and that's before accounting for how tired you'll be after a full day of loading a truck. Black ice through the Berkshires or an accident-clogged I-84 in Connecticut can turn an eight-hour drive into a fourteen-hour one, fast. If your move date lands anywhere near a storm system, or you're simply not up for a multi-day drive on top of everything else moving involves, shipping buys back time and energy you'll need for the actual move-in.

## Boston's Narrow Streets Change Your Pickup Plan

Auto carriers are big rigs, 75 feet or longer when fully loaded, and that size doesn't play well with Boston's tighter residential streets. A triple-decker block in Somerville or Allston, or a one-way stretch in JP, usually isn't wide enough for a car hauler to pull up and load.

What actually happens is the driver calls ahead once they're close and proposes a nearby meeting spot instead: a shopping plaza lot, a wide arterial street, or a commercial block within a few minutes of your address. This is standard practice across the industry, not a Boston-specific inconvenience, but it catches people off guard here more than in cities built around wider streets. Plan for it. Pick a backup meeting point before pickup day so you're not scrambling to find one with a carrier idling nearby and the meter running on a driver who's behind schedule.

## How to Avoid Auto Transport Scams

The same red flags that show up in [moving company scams](/blog/moving-company-scams-massachusetts) show up in car shipping, too: a lowball quote that seems too good, pressure to wire a deposit immediately, and vague answers about who's actually driving your car.

Before you book, verify the carrier's MC number through FMCSA's SAFER system, and confirm they're registered the way [FMCSA's Protect Your Move program](https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/protect-your-move) recommends for any interstate transporter. Get your pickup and delivery windows in writing, and be wary of any broker who won't name the carrier until after you've paid. A legitimate company will tell you exactly who's showing up and when, not just promise it'll work out. If a "dispatcher" calls from a personal cell number and can't produce a company name and DOT number on request, that's your answer.

## When Should You Book Your Car Shipment?

Book 2-4 weeks ahead for most routes. If your move lands anywhere near September 1, Boston's [biggest moving day of the year](/blog/moving-september-1-boston), push that to 4-6 weeks, since car carriers get pulled toward the same peak-season demand that maxes out moving trucks. The same applies to summer generally; more people move, more cars need shipping, and availability tightens across the board. Booking a moving crew follows [similar timing rules](/blog/how-far-in-advance-to-book-movers-boston), so it's worth locking both in during the same week of planning instead of treating them as separate errands.

## What to Check Before You Sign for Delivery

Whatever you ship, inspect it before you sign anything. Walk the full car, check panels and wheels against any photos or notes you took at pickup, and note anything new directly on the delivery paperwork before the driver leaves. Once you sign a clean delivery receipt, disputing damage after the fact gets a lot harder.

Keep your pickup inspection notes and photos until the car's back in your hands and you've had a full look in daylight, not just under a truck driver's flashlight at 7pm.
