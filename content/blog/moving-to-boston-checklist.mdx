Short version: Massachusetts banned mandatory tenant broker fees in 2025, you have 30 days to register a car once you're a resident, and September 1 is the one date on the calendar worth planning around. Here's the full checklist for moving to Boston from out of state.

## What Changed for Renters Moving to Boston in 2026

If you researched Boston's rental market even a year ago, you probably read that tenants get stuck paying a broker fee equal to a full month's rent, on top of first month, last month, and a security deposit. That's no longer automatically true. A [Massachusetts law that took effect August 1, 2025](https://www.mass.gov/news/governor-healey-forced-renter-paid-brokers-fees-end-today) shifted responsibility for broker fees to whoever hires the broker. If your landlord lists the unit through an agent, the landlord pays that agent, not you.

You'll still owe a fee if you hire your own broker to help hunt for an apartment, which some newcomers do anyway for the local knowledge. But the default cost of moving into Boston just dropped by roughly a month's rent for a huge share of renters, and a lot of older blog posts and guides haven't caught up to that change yet. Budget first month, last month, and a security deposit, each legally capped at one month's rent, and treat any broker fee as optional rather than assumed.

## Do You Actually Need a Car Here?

Probably not, if you're landing in the core neighborhoods. Back Bay, Beacon Hill, the South End, the North End, Cambridge, and most of Somerville are dense enough to walk, and the T covers the rest. A [Monthly LinkPass runs $90](https://www.mbta.com/fares) and covers subway, local bus, and Silver Line access — cheaper than most cities' equivalent pass, and dramatically cheaper than a car payment plus Boston parking.

Outside that core (parts of Dorchester, the outer suburbs, anywhere the Green Line doesn't reach), the math flips, and a car stops being optional. Either way, decide before you move, not after your first month of alternate-side parking tickets makes the decision for you. If you're bringing a car regardless of the neighborhood, read on, because Massachusetts doesn't give you much runway to sort out its paperwork.

## Registering Your Car and Getting a Resident Parking Permit

You have 30 days from establishing Massachusetts residency to [register your vehicle with the RMV](https://www.mass.gov/how-to/transfer-your-registration-and-title-from-out-of-state). There's no grace period beyond that window, and the process has a specific order: get Massachusetts insurance first, then bring your out-of-state title, an odometer disclosure statement, and proof of Boston residency (a lease or a utility bill works) to complete the transfer. Budget for a vehicle inspection within 7 days of registering, too; it's a separate step people forget until the sticker on their old plates expires.

Once your registration shows a Boston address, apply for a [resident parking permit](https://www.boston.gov/departments/parking-clerk/resident-parking-permits) through the city. It's free, but it's issued by parking zone rather than citywide, so it only covers the specific neighborhood where you're registered — move again later and you'll need a new one. Bring proof of residency and make sure you don't have unpaid tickets on file, since Boston won't issue a permit until those are cleared.

## How Do You Pick a Neighborhood Sight Unseen?

Plenty of newcomers sign a lease off photos and a map, then discover the building's fourth-floor walk-up or the block's total absence of parking after the truck is already loaded. Boston's neighborhoods vary more than most cities on exactly the details that matter for move-in day: staircase width, elevator access, how far the truck can actually park from your door. [Some Boston neighborhoods make moving genuinely harder than others](/blog/hardest-boston-neighborhoods-to-move): narrow one-way streets in Beacon Hill and the North End, resident-only parking in most of Back Bay and the South End. It's worth knowing which ones before you're standing in front of a triple-decker with no legal place for a 26-foot truck to sit.

If you're choosing between neighborhoods, ask about building age and access as seriously as you'd ask about rent. A ground-floor unit with a loading dock and a fourth-floor walk-up in a building built in 1910 are not the same move, even at an identical price point. Before you sign anything, ask the landlord or leasing office directly:

- Is there an elevator, and does it fit a mattress or a couch?
- Can a moving truck park within reasonable carrying distance of the entrance?
- Is the street resident-permit parking, and if so, which zone?
- What floor is the unit on, and how wide is the stairwell?

## Why September 1 Should Be on Your Radar, Even in March

Most newcomers have never heard of it, and then they learn fast. [September 1 is Boston's mass lease-turnover date](/blog/moving-september-1-boston), driven by the sheer number of colleges and standardized 12-month leases clustered around the same start date. If your move happens to land in the week surrounding it, expect movers booked out weeks in advance, parking permits harder to secure, and prices meaningfully higher than the rest of the year.

If your timeline has any flexibility at all, [January through March tends to run cheaper](/blog/best-time-of-year-to-move-boston) across the board, since demand drops sharply after the September rush. That's not always realistic for a job relocation or a lease that starts on a fixed date, but if you do have a choice, avoiding the week around September 1 is worth more than almost any other single scheduling decision you'll make.

## What Do You Need to Set Up Before You Arrive?

Get your electric, gas, and internet accounts set up before you arrive, not after. Most of the city runs on Eversource for electric and National Grid for gas, though the exact provider depends on your specific address, and a same-day connection isn't guaranteed if you call the morning you land. File your USPS mail forwarding at least a week ahead, and give yourself the full [change-of-address checklist](/blog/change-of-address-checklist-boston) rather than assuming forwarding alone covers it — it doesn't update your bank, your employer, or the RMV, all of which need to hear from you directly.

Trash and recycling pickup runs by zone in Boston, not citywide, so your collection day resets the moment you move in. Check it during your first week rather than guessing, or you'll find out the hard way with a bag sitting on the curb for six extra days.

## What Does a Newcomer's Move Actually Look Like?

Moving into Boston from out of state is a different job than a local move across town, and it's worth knowing that before you book anyone. You're usually coordinating a delivery window from an interstate carrier or a one-way truck rental with a local crew that unloads it, rather than one company handling both ends door to door. That handoff point — truck shows up, someone has to actually get everything up the stairs and into the unit — is where a lot of newcomer moves go sideways, especially into a walk-up nobody measured in advance.

We see this pattern constantly with people landing in Boston for the first time: the move itself goes fine, but the building details, no freight elevator reserved, no permit for where the truck needs to park, weren't sorted until the truck was already idling outside. Locking in your unload crew and confirming building access before your delivery window arrives, not the morning of, is the difference between an easy afternoon and a stressful one.
