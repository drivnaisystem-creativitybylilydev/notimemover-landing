Short answer: hazardous materials, perishable food, live plants, pets, and irreplaceable valuables. Movers won't take them, and in most cases they legally can't. Here's the full list, why it exists, and what to actually do with the stuff that's left in your hands on moving day.

## The Full List: What Boston Movers Won't (or Can't) Take

Every legitimate moving company in the country runs some version of the same non-allowable items list, because most of it traces back to federal hazmat transport rules rather than a company's individual preference. [Move.org's breakdown of what movers won't move](https://www.move.org/moving-companies-not-move/) covers the standard categories, and Boston movers follow the same baseline.

| Category | Examples | Why It's Off the Truck |
|---|---|---|
| Hazardous materials | Propane tanks, gasoline, oil-based paint, pool chemicals, fireworks, ammunition, aerosol cans | Federal transport rules bar movers from hauling flammables, corrosives, and compressed gas |
| Perishables | Fresh or frozen food, anything requiring refrigeration | No climate control in a truck, and spoilage risk over a multi-hour or multi-day haul |
| Live plants | Any potted or rooted plant | Temperature swings and darkness in a closed truck kill most plants in transit anyway |
| Pets | Dogs, cats, fish, birds, any live animal | No mover is set up or insured to transport live animals safely |
| Valuables and irreplaceables | Cash, jewelry, passports, medications, hard drives, family photos, important documents | Liability coverage caps mean a lost box isn't reimbursed anywhere close to what these items are actually worth |

None of this is arbitrary. A crew that agrees to haul a full propane tank isn't being flexible, it's taking on a liability no insurance policy is built to cover, and it's putting your other belongings at risk if something in that truck ignites.

## Why Can't Movers Just Take These Anyway?

The honest answer is liability, not laziness. [Fully insured movers](/blog/what-fully-insured-means-boston-movers) carry coverage sized around furniture, boxes, and household goods, not compressed gas or flammable liquid. A propane tank that shifts during transit and punctures isn't a claim any policy is priced to absorb, and a crew that knowingly loads one is exposing themselves, you, and everyone else's belongings on that truck to the same risk.

Interstate movers face this as actual federal law, not just company policy. Intrastate Boston-to-Boston jobs have more gray area technically, but every operator worth hiring applies the same standard regardless, because the risk doesn't change based on which side of a state line the truck crosses.

None of this means the items themselves have to stay behind. You're allowed to transport most of them yourself, in your own car, in small quantities. A can of paint thinner or a bottle of pool chemicals can ride in a trunk with a window cracked. A propane tank is a different situation. It needs to stay upright and ventilated, which means an open trunk or a truck bed, never a sealed passenger cabin where a slow leak has nowhere to go.

## E-Bikes and Scooter Batteries Are the New Gray Area

This one is newer, and it's catching people off guard. Lithium-ion batteries, the kind in e-bikes, scooters, and hoverboards, can enter thermal runaway if punctured, crushed, or overcharged, which means they can ignite without warning hours after they were packed and loaded. It's not a theoretical risk. The [Consumer Product Safety Commission proposed the first mandatory federal safety standard](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/06/24/2026-12749/safety-standard-for-lithium-ion-batteries-used-in-micromobility-products-and-electrical-systems-of) for micromobility batteries in June 2026, citing 227 fire, explosion, and overheating incidents tracked over a five-year period, including dozens of associated deaths.

E-bike ownership in Boston has grown fast enough that most crews now ask about it directly during booking, rather than discovering a battery pack halfway through loading a truck. If you own one, plan to transport the battery pack yourself, separately from the bike frame, in your own vehicle. The frame itself is fine on the truck. It's specifically the battery that's the problem.

## What About Plants, Pets, and Perishable Food?

These three get lumped together because the reasoning is similar: none of them survive a closed truck with no climate control, and none of them are worth the risk of trying. A box of frozen food sitting in a hot truck for six hours isn't salvageable by the time it arrives. A fern in the dark for a full day of transit usually doesn't make it either.

Pets are the one people ask about most, and the answer is always the same: they ride with you, not the truck, full stop. If you're [moving with a dog or cat](/blog/moving-with-pets-boston), plan for them to be in a carrier in your own car on moving day, ideally somewhere quiet and away from the noise of a crew hauling furniture past them all morning.

## Valuables That Should Ride With You, Not the Truck

This is the category people forget most often, mostly because it doesn't look dangerous the way a propane tank does. Cash, jewelry, passports, prescription medications, external hard drives, and anything genuinely irreplaceable like family photos or a box of important documents should travel in your car, not the truck.

The reasoning is liability, same as everything else on this list. Standard mover coverage caps out at a per-pound rate, not the actual value of what's inside a box, so a lost envelope of cash or a missing box of jewelry isn't made whole the way a damaged dresser might be. Keep one bag or box of exactly this category with you at all times on moving day. It's a five-minute habit that removes the single most stressful "what if" of the entire process.

## Where to Actually Get Rid of Hazardous Items in Boston

Knowing what's banned doesn't solve the actual problem: you're still standing in your apartment with a half-full propane tank and a can of old deck stain the week of your move. Here's where it actually goes.

Propane tanks are the easiest fix. Home Depot, Lowe's, and most Ace Hardware locations around Greater Boston run tank exchange programs, swap the empty for a full one, or drop off an empty tank for a small fee if you don't need a refill. Paint depends on the type. Dried-out latex or acrylic paint can go straight in your regular household trash once it's solidified, but oil-based paint needs proper disposal, not the trash can. Boston's Public Works Department runs [Zero Waste Day drop-off events](https://www.boston.gov/departments/public-works/zero-waste-day) throughout the year that accept oil-based paint, batteries, electronics, and household chemicals, generally with a 20-gallon limit per household. Check the current schedule before your move date, since these run periodically rather than continuously.

Pool chemicals, old batteries, and cleaning chemicals under the sink fall into the same Zero Waste Day category. If your move date doesn't line up with a scheduled drop-off, most of these items keep fine in a garage or storage closet for a few extra weeks until the next one, which beats trying to sneak them onto a truck.

## What Happens If You Pack a Banned Item by Accident?

It happens more than people expect, usually with something that doesn't register as hazardous, a half-used can of spray paint at the back of a closet, or a bottle of pool chemicals left over from summer. A good crew pulls it aside rather than loading it and lets you know so you can deal with it separately, no drama involved.

Crews aren't opening every sealed box to check what's inside, and they're not expected to. What actually gets caught is the obvious stuff: a box that's leaking, one that smells like gasoline or solvent, or one labeled in a way that doesn't match what's clearly inside it. The honor system works because most people aren't trying to hide anything. Telling your crew about anything questionable before they touch it beats hoping it slides by unnoticed.

Where it becomes an actual problem is when hazmat gets hidden inside a sealed, unlabeled box, whether on purpose or by accident. If something leaks, ignites, or gets discovered mid-load, that box can hold up loading for everyone else on the job, and depending on what it is, it can be genuinely dangerous for the crew handling it. If you're [decluttering before your move](/blog/declutter-before-a-boston-move) anyway, sorting anything remotely hazardous into its own clearly labeled pile is worth the extra ten minutes. It's the same instinct that makes a [well-organized packing job](/blog/packing-guide-boston-apartment) faster for your crew generally, just applied to the one category where getting it wrong actually matters.
