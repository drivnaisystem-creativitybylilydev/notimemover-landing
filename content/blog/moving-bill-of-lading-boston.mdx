Your bill of lading isn't paperwork you skim on the truck ramp and sign so the crew can get moving. It's the actual contract for your move, and once it's signed, you're largely stuck with whatever's on it.

## What a Bill of Lading Actually Is

Strip away the legal-sounding name and it's simple: a receipt for your shipment and the contract governing how it gets moved, combined into a single document. Every mover, whether they're driving your boxes across town or across state lines, has to prepare one for every job they run. No exceptions for a "quick local move" or a job booked the week before.

It lists what's being transported, where it's coming from and going to, what you agreed to pay, and which valuation coverage applies if something breaks. If a dispute ever happens, [this is the first document an adjuster or regulator pulls](/blog/moving-damage-claim-boston). Not your original estimate, not a text exchange with the crew lead. The signed bill of lading.

Most people treat it as a formality, something you sign because the crew is standing there waiting and the truck is already half-loaded. That's exactly the wrong moment to be reading it for the first time.

## Order for Service vs. Move-Day Paperwork: What's the Difference?

These two get confused constantly, and the confusion causes real problems. The order for service is what you sign when you book, the document that turns your estimate into a formal agreement covering price, dates, and what's included. The bill of lading is what the crew has you sign on move day itself, once your belongings are actually being loaded onto the truck.

They're supposed to say the same thing. Massachusetts regulations governing movers require the [order for service and the bill of lading to match](https://www.mass.gov/info-details/moving-companies-regulated-by-the-department-of-public-utilities-dpu), and the same principle holds for interstate moves under federal rules. If the price, the inventory, or the payment terms on the truck-day document don't line up with what you signed at booking, that gap is the whole ballgame. It's either an honest mistake worth flagging immediately, or it's the moment a lowball quote turns into a real bill.

## Is It Legally Binding?

Yes, and this is the part most people underestimate. [Federal guidance on this is blunt](https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/protect-your-move/regulations-and-enforcement): it's your responsibility to understand the bill of lading before you sign it, and you shouldn't sign until you're satisfied it's correct. That's not boilerplate. Once your signature is on it, you're mostly bound by its terms, and disputing something you signed off on is a much harder conversation than catching it beforehand.

This cuts both ways. It protects you, because a mover generally can't add charges that aren't reflected anywhere on the signed document. It also limits you, because "I didn't really read it" isn't a strong position once a disagreement lands in front of a regulator or a claims adjuster. Read it before you sign. Not after the truck pulls away.

## What Should Be on Every Bill of Lading

The specifics vary slightly by mover, but a legitimate bill of lading should always include the following, whether your move is crossing state lines or staying inside Route 128.

| Field | What to Verify |
|---|---|
| Mover identification | Company name, address, and USDOT number (interstate) or DPU registration (local) |
| Your information | Your name, phone number, and both addresses |
| Pickup and delivery dates | Match what was agreed on the order for service |
| Itemized inventory | Reflects what's actually being loaded, not a rough estimate |
| Valuation/coverage selected | Released value or full value protection, clearly marked |
| Payment terms | Total amount, form of payment, and when it's due |

If any of these fields are blank, vague, or filled in with something that doesn't match your order for service, that's the moment to ask questions, not sign and hope it works itself out.

## What Interstate Moves Require Under FMCSA Rules

If you're [moving out of Massachusetts](/long-distance-moving), your bill of lading falls under federal rules enforced by the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration. The carrier is required to hand you a copy at pickup, and it has to reflect the same form of payment you agreed to on your estimate, not a different arrangement the driver prefers on the day.

Interstate movers also have to provide a consumer rights booklet, [FMCSA's "Your Rights and Responsibilities When You Move"](https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/sites/fmcsa.dot.gov/files/RightsandResponsibilitiesBooklet_2022Update.pdf), which walks through your protections around estimates, valuation, and claims. Almost nobody reads it cover to cover, understandably, but it's worth knowing it exists and that you're entitled to it before your stuff is on the truck.

This is also the document your nine-month window to [file a damage claim](/blog/moving-damage-claim-boston) runs from. The delivery date stamped on your bill of lading is the clock's start line, so keep a copy somewhere you won't lose it.

## Does the Same Paperwork Apply to a Local Boston Move?

Not exactly, and this is the part most national moving guides skip entirely, since they're written with interstate moves in mind. A move that starts and ends inside Massachusetts isn't regulated by the federal government at all. It falls under the state's Department of Public Utilities, and the paperwork rules run through DPU regulations rather than FMCSA's.

Practically, the bill of lading itself looks similar: mover info, addresses, inventory, valuation, payment terms. What changes is what you're checking. Instead of a US DOT number, [verify the mover's DPU registration](/blog/how-to-choose-a-moving-company-boston) rather than taking "we're insured" at face value. And instead of the interstate consumer rights booklet, Massachusetts imposes its own written-estimate and matching-documentation requirements on movers operating in-state.

A lot of Boston jobs get treated informally because they're "just crosstown," a move from Somerville to the South End that feels too small to need real paperwork. The regulations don't see it that way, and neither should you. A one-day local move still gets a real bill of lading, and it still deserves an actual read before you sign it.

## What to Check Before You Sign

Before the crew has you sign anything on move day, run through this quickly. It takes two minutes and it's the cheapest insurance policy available to you.

- Does the total match your order for service, or has it quietly moved?
- Do the pickup and delivery dates match what you agreed to?
- Is the inventory count roughly what's actually going on the truck, not a placeholder number from booking?
- Is your valuation coverage marked correctly, released value or full value protection?
- Are any [extra charges](/blog/hidden-moving-fees-boston), stairs, long carry, parking, actually written on the document, not just mentioned by the crew lead?

If something's off, say so before the truck leaves the curb, not after. A mover correcting a line item on the spot is routine. Getting it fixed after delivery, with everyone's memory of the conversation, is much harder.

## What If You Catch a Mistake After You've Signed?

Two different situations, two different fixes. If you catch an error while the crew is still on-site, loading or about to leave, ask for a correction and a re-signature before they go. Reputable crews handle this without friction; it happens more often than you'd think.

If you don't catch it until later, delivery day or after, the bill of lading becomes the document you're working from rather than one you're still negotiating. That's the point where [a written damage or discrepancy claim](/blog/moving-damage-claim-boston) is the right tool, not an attempt to renegotiate a signed contract after the fact. Note discrepancies in writing, keep your copy of the bill of lading with the delivery date visible, and start the claims process rather than sitting on it.
