Short answer: moving with a newborn in Boston works like any other move, plus one legal step almost nobody mentions. Here's the full plan, safety details included.

## The One Legal Step Most Moving Guides Skip

National moving guides tell you to pack a go-bag and call the pediatrician. Fine advice, but none of it accounts for where you're actually moving to. In Massachusetts, if you're relocating into housing built before 1978 and a child under 6 will live there, [the state's Lead Law](https://www.mass.gov/info-details/learn-about-massachusetts-lead-law) applies, and a huge share of Boston's triple-deckers, walk-ups, and older apartment stock falls into that category without anyone thinking twice about it.

The obligation sits with the owner, not you. A new owner taking title on pre-1978 housing where a child under 6 will live has 90 days to either delead the unit or get [a Letter of Interim Control](https://www.mass.gov/info-details/learn-about-interim-control-of-lead-paint-hazards), which addresses urgent hazards on a temporary basis. If you're renting, the landlord has to give you a Tenant Lead Law Notification before you sign the lease, regardless of whether you have a baby yet. Ask for it directly if it doesn't show up on its own.

| Step | Requirement |
|---|---|
| New owner takes title (pre-1978, child under 6 will live there) | Delead or get a Letter of Interim Control within 90 days |
| Letter of Interim Control | Valid 1 year, renewable once for a 2nd year |
| Full deleading (Letter of Compliance) | Required by end of year 2 if a child under 6 still lives there |
| Tenant Lead Law Notification | Owner must provide before lease signing, baby or not |

None of this should scare you off an older apartment. It just means asking the landlord one extra question before you sign, the same way you'd ask about parking or laundry.

## Is There a Good Age to Move a Newborn?

There's no ideal age, but there's a hardest one. The first 6-8 weeks after birth are the roughest window to layer a move on top of, between physical recovery and a newborn who isn't on any kind of schedule yet. If you're [still pregnant and have any control over timing](/blog/moving-while-pregnant-boston), moving before your due date is usually easier than moving in that early postpartum stretch.

Not everyone gets that choice. Leases turn over, jobs relocate people, and Boston's rental calendar doesn't wait for a baby's schedule. If you're moving in those first weeks regardless, build in more help than you think you need, and don't expect to do any of the physical work yourself.

## Should Your Baby Be There on Moving Day?

Ideally, no. A sitter, grandparent, or friend taking the baby somewhere quiet during loading and unloading beats holding or wearing them while a crew is carrying furniture through a stairwell. It's not just about noise and chaos, it's genuinely safer to keep a baby out of a space where people are moving heavy items past narrow landings, which describes most Boston walk-up stairwells on a normal day.

The car seat matters here too. [MoveBuddha's guidance is specific on this](https://www.movebuddha.com/blog/car-seat-moving-truck/): keep it installed in the back seat of whatever vehicle is transporting your baby, not loose in the moving truck's cargo area. A car seat that rode in a truck isn't something you want to trust again even if nothing looks obviously wrong with it.

## Pack the Nursery Last, Set It Up First

Everything else in your apartment can wait a day to get unpacked. The crib can't, not if you want a familiar sleep setup on night one instead of a pack-and-play improvised at 11pm. Keep the nursery intact until the very last thing goes on the truck, and tell whoever's leading your crew that it needs to come off first.

Getting it right matters more than it might seem. The [American Academy of Pediatrics recommends](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/sleep/Pages/a-parents-guide-to-safe-sleep.aspx) a firm, flat mattress with no pillows, blankets, or soft bedding, and room-sharing (not bed-sharing) for at least the first 6 months, which can cut SIDS risk by roughly half. That's exactly the setup that gets rushed or skipped during a move, when a crib ends up half-assembled in a corner while everyone deals with boxes instead.

## The Newborn Go-Bag: Pack It Separately

Treat this bag like carry-on luggage, not cargo. It travels with a parent, never on the truck, and it doesn't get touched by the crew.

- Diapers, wipes, and a change of clothes for at least a full day
- Formula and bottles, or pumping supplies and stored milk
- A swaddle or sleep sack, plus a pacifier if your baby uses one
- Any medications, with dosing instructions
- A copy of the vaccination record and your pediatrician's contact info

If your move stretches into a second day for any reason, this bag is the difference between managing fine and scrambling through unlabeled boxes at 2am.

## How Do You Transfer Your Baby's Medical Records?

Start as soon as you have a new address, not after you've settled in. Under [HIPAA](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/faq/right-to-access-medical-records/index.html), your current pediatrician's office has 30 days to send records once you request them, with one 30-day extension allowed if they notify you in writing, so the outer limit is roughly 60 days in a slow case. Ask for the full record, immunizations included, and keep your own copy once it arrives instead of relying on the new office to have everything transferred cleanly.

If your family is on MassHealth, switching your child's primary care provider is a separate step from the records request. The old provider isn't automatically notified when you change, and the switch isn't instant, so keep using your current pediatrician until the new one is confirmed active rather than assuming coverage transfers the moment you move.

## Babyproofing a Triple-Decker on a Baby's Timeline

Most babies start crawling somewhere between 4 and 10 months, with 6-10 the more typical range. That gives you a real window to work with, but it closes faster than people expect once a baby who couldn't move six weeks ago is suddenly everywhere.

Handle the big stuff early: furniture anchored to the wall, outlet covers in, anything toppleable secured before it needs to be. Boston's older buildings add a few things a suburban babyproofing checklist won't mention. Cast-iron radiators in pre-war triple-deckers get hot enough to matter and usually aren't covered by anything. Stairwells in a walk-up are steeper and narrower than what most gates are designed for, so measure before you buy one. Older double-hung windows, common across Allston, Dorchester, and Somerville's triple-decker stock, often don't have the modern stops or locks a newer building would, worth checking alongside [anything else on your move-in checklist](/blog/move-in-checklist-boston-apartment) before your baby is mobile enough to test them.

If you've done this once already with an older child, this stage moves faster the second time.
