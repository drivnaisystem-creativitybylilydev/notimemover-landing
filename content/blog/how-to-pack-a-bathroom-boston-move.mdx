A bathroom is the smallest room in a Boston apartment and somehow still the one people get wrong. Here's how to pack it in under an hour without losing a bottle of shampoo down the stairwell.

## Should You Pack the Bathroom First or Last?

Last, and it's not close. Every other room can get boxed up over a week or two, but a bathroom has to stay functional until the truck actually pulls away. Pack it too early and you're digging through sealed boxes for a toothbrush the night before a move. That defeats the entire point of packing ahead.

Treat the bathroom as a 20-30 minute job on moving morning itself, not a project to spread across your packing timeline. Set aside your moving day bag first, thing by thing, then box up everything left over. That order matters more here than in any other room in the apartment.

## What Actually Needs a Box?

Less than you'd think. A typical bathroom fits into 2-3 medium boxes: one for extra towels and linens, one for toiletries and backup supplies, and one for cleaning products if you're not tossing them before the move. A shared bathroom with a family's worth of backup shampoo and first-aid supplies stacked under the sink can push closer to 4, but it rarely goes beyond that.

Sort what you're packing into three groups before a single box gets taped shut:

- Daily-use items that go in your moving day bag, not a box at all
- Backup and rarely-used items that get boxed normally
- Anything half-empty, expired, or hazardous that doesn't travel on the truck

That third category is where most bathroom-packing mistakes happen, and it's worth its own section.

Here's roughly how it breaks down by category:

| Category | How to Pack It | Box Type |
|---|---|---|
| Towels and linens | Fold flat, use as padding for glass items | Medium box |
| Daily toiletries | Skip the box, goes in your moving day bag | None |
| Backup toiletries and liquids | Seal caps with plastic wrap, stand upright | Small plastic bin |
| Cleaning supplies | Check for aerosols/corrosives first, box the rest | Medium box |
| Medications | Sort, dispose of what's expired, keep the rest with you | Personal bag |
| Hazmat (aerosols, nail polish remover, drain cleaner) | Use up, give away, or dispose of before moving day | Not on the truck |

## How Do You Keep Liquids From Leaking in the Truck?

Tighten every cap as far as it goes, then take it back off. Put a small square of plastic wrap over the bottle's opening, and screw the cap down over that. It sounds like overkill until a cap works loose somewhere between your third-floor walk-up and the truck, and a full bottle of conditioner isn't the thing you want to discover has leaked into a box of towels.

Pack liquids upright in a plastic bin instead of a cardboard box when you can — a leak stays contained in plastic, where a cardboard box just soaks it in and passes it to whatever's underneath. [This Old House's guide to packing liquids](https://www.thisoldhouse.com/moving/how-to-pack-liquids-for-moving) recommends bagging anything already half-used or prone to seeping, then nesting bagged bottles into any empty gaps so they can't tip and roll during the drive.

Glass items, like a soap dispenser, a mirror-backed medicine cabinet shelf, or perfume bottles, get wrapped the same way you'd wrap [any other fragile item](/blog/packing-fragile-items-boston-move): in a towel, not loose bubble wrap. That does double duty since you're packing towels anyway, and it means one fewer supply to buy before moving day.

## What Won't Movers Put on the Truck?

More of your bathroom than you'd expect. Federal hazmat transport rules apply to aerosol cans, nail polish remover, rubbing alcohol, and drain cleaner, the same rules that keep [propane tanks and paint off a moving truck](/blog/what-movers-wont-move-boston). None of it is a Boston-specific quirk or a NoTimeMover policy. Every legitimate mover in the country runs the same list, because it traces back to federal transport regulations rather than any one company's preference.

Practically, that means hairspray, aerosol dry shampoo, and anything flammable or corrosive needs to either get used up in the days before your move, handed off to a neighbor, or thrown out through your building's normal trash pickup. None of it gets packed with everything else and handed to the crew, no matter how small the can is.

## Where to Get Rid of Old Medications Before You Pack

This is the one most guides skip entirely, and it matters more in Boston than most cities give it credit for. The Boston Police Department runs [MedReturn drug collection kiosks at every district station](https://www.boston.gov/departments/boston-public-health-commission/medication-and-syringe-disposal), open 24 hours a day, seven days a week, no appointment and no questions asked. It's the fastest legitimate way to clear out a medicine cabinet full of old prescriptions before a move, and it's free.

If a district station isn't on your way, the [FDA's guidance on medication disposal](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/where-and-how-dispose-unused-medicines) is the fallback: mix pills with dirt, cat litter, or used coffee grounds in a sealed bag, then throw the bag out with your regular trash. Don't flush anything unless the label specifically says to — most medications shouldn't go down a drain, and Boston's water system isn't built to filter pharmaceuticals out before it reaches the harbor.

Do this a few days before moving day, not the morning of. It's a five-minute errand, but it's the kind of five minutes that gets skipped entirely once the truck shows up.

Sharps, like used insulin needles or lancets, need their own path. MedReturn kiosks are built for pills, not needles, so those go in a rigid, sealed container and get dropped at a participating Boston pharmacy instead, most of which are required to take sharps back at no cost under a 2020 city ordinance. Don't toss them loose in a bathroom trash bag, moving or not.

## Small Bathrooms, Old Buildings: The Boston Wrinkle

Boston's [older housing stock](/blog/packing-guide-boston-apartment) shows up hardest in the bathroom. A lot of triple-deckers and pre-war buildings still have the original medicine cabinet built into the wall — shallow, mirror-fronted, and usually the single most over-stuffed storage spot in the entire apartment. Crews packing out of these units regularly find years of half-used toiletries wedged behind whatever's actually in daily use, which is exactly the stuff that should have been tossed at the first sorting pass, not carried to a new address.

There's also just less floor space to stage boxes in a typical Boston bathroom than in a newer build. Pack directly into the box on the counter or the closed toilet lid rather than trying to sort things on the floor first. In a lot of these units, there isn't floor space to spare, and a half-packed box balanced on the edge of a clawfoot tub is asking for trouble.

We see this pattern on nearly every walk-up job: the rooms people assume will be quick, like the bathroom, are the ones that eat the most last-minute time because nobody planned for them. Locking in a flat price before the truck shows up doesn't change how much there is to pack, but it does mean a bathroom that takes 45 minutes instead of 20 isn't turning into a surprise line item on your bill.

## Your Moving Day Toiletries Bag

Pack a small bag with what you'll actually touch before bed on night one and again the next morning: toothbrush and toothpaste, any daily medications, contacts or a glasses case, deodorant, and a roll of toilet paper. Keep it with you in the car, not on the truck — it belongs alongside the rest of your [moving day essentials](/blog/moving-day-essentials-box-boston), not buried in a box you won't open until the weekend.

It's a short list on paper, but it's the difference between a functional first night and rifling through sealed boxes at 11pm looking for a toothbrush.
