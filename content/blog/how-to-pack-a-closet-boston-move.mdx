Short answer: budget one wardrobe box for every two feet of clothes actually hanging on your closet rod. Here's how to measure that instead of guessing, and what's cheaper left folded.

## Do You Actually Need Wardrobe Boxes?

Not for everything in there. Wardrobe boxes exist for clothes that lose their shape or wrinkle badly when folded: suits, dresses, coats, anything structured. A wool blazer crushed into a duffel bag for two days shows it. A folded t-shirt doesn't.

For the rest of your closet, skip the wardrobe box. T-shirts, jeans, sweaters, and athletic wear fold fine into standard boxes, and folded clothes actually pack tighter than hanging ones, since there's no wasted air space around each garment. [U-Haul's wardrobe box lineup](https://www.uhaul.com/MovingSupplies/Clothing-Moving-Boxes/) runs $8 to $21 depending on the size, so buying six of them for a closet that only needed two is money you didn't have to spend.

The honest split: if it hangs on a hanger at home because folding it would ruin it, it goes in a wardrobe box. If it hangs on a hanger out of habit, fold it.

## How Many Wardrobe Boxes Do You Actually Need?

Measure your closet rod, not your closet. The rule that holds up in practice: one wardrobe box per two feet of clothes actually hanging on the bar. A crammed 4-foot rod of dress shirts and blazers needs two boxes. A sparse 4-foot rod with a handful of coats might only need one.

Round up, not down. Overpacking a wardrobe box to save $17 means clothes get crushed against the sides anyway, which defeats the point of buying the box in the first place.

| Closet Type | Typical Rod Length | Wardrobe Boxes Needed |
|---|---|---|
| Studio closet | 3-4 ft | 1-2 |
| One-bedroom closet | 4-6 ft | 2-3 |
| Two-bedroom (primary + guest) | 8-10 ft | 4-5 |
| Walk-in closet | 10-14 ft | 5-7 |

Standard and large wardrobe boxes hold up to 100 pounds and run roughly $17-$21 each. A shorter "laydown" box, meant for folded coats or kids' clothes rather than a full hanging rod, costs closer to $8. Mixing sizes based on what's actually in each closet is cheaper than buying one size for the whole apartment.

Don't toss the boxes once you're unpacked, either. Broken down flat and kept dry, a wardrobe box survives a few more moves before the cardboard gives out, though the metal hanging bar usually bends first if it gets crushed under other boxes in storage.

## What to Hang vs. What to Fold

Hang anything structured or delicate: suits, blazers, dresses, silk, anything with a shoulder shape that folding would ruin. Fold everything else. T-shirts, jeans, sweatshirts, and workout clothes stack cleanly in regular boxes and take up noticeably less truck space than the same items hanging in a wardrobe box's dead air.

Puffy or bulky items, winter coats and down jackets especially, do better hung in a garment bag inside the wardrobe box than folded, since folding compresses the insulation and they never quite puff back out the same. For delicate natural fibers like silk or cashmere that are staying folded, a layer of tissue paper between pieces keeps them from snagging on each other in the box.

## Packing Shoes So They Don't Wreck Everything Else

Shoes are the one item that damages other things in the box, not itself. Hard soles and heels catch on fabric, scuff leather, and snag delicate weaves if they're loose among your clothes.

Pack shoes in their original boxes when you kept them, or wrap each pair individually in paper and group them in their own box or the bottom layer of a wardrobe box, never mixed in with hanging garments. Stuff paper or socks inside each shoe first so they hold their shape instead of collapsing under the weight of everything stacked on top. This is the same logic behind [ConsumerAffairs' clothes-packing guide](https://www.consumeraffairs.com/movers/how-to-pack-clothes-for-moving.html): pack in pairs, keep shoes separate from soft goods, and clean them before boxing so you're not moving street dirt into a new closet.

## Should Off-Season Clothes Even Make the Move?

Probably not into your new closet, at least not right away. If it's September and your winter coats are packed, there's no reason they need to come off the truck before your new bedroom furniture does. Boxing off-season clothes separately and routing them straight to storage, or just leaving them boxed until the season actually turns, saves you from unpacking something you won't touch for months.

[PODS' guide to seasonal clothing storage](https://www.pods.com/blog/6-tips-for-storing-off-season-clothes) recommends clear, sealed plastic bins over cardboard for anything sitting for a while, since bins hold up better against moisture and pests in a basement or storage unit. Natural fibers like wool and silk want a little air circulation rather than a fully sealed bag, so a canvas bin works better for those than plastic. If you're already paying for a [storage unit during the move](/blog/boston-storage-options-during-a-move), off-season clothes are one of the easiest categories to route there directly instead of double-handling them.

## Boston Closets Are Smaller Than the Listing Photos

Closet space is where Boston apartments quietly disappoint people who moved from anywhere with more square footage. A lot of triple-deckers and pre-war walk-ups in Somerville, Allston, Jamaica Plain, and parts of Cambridge were built before reach-in closets were standard, so what's there is often a single narrow closet per bedroom, sometimes doing double duty as linen storage too.

That's why the rod-measurement approach in this guide matters more here than a national moving guide's generic advice. A "one bedroom" in a Back Bay brownstone and a "one bedroom" in a suburban new-build can have wildly different closet capacity, even though the listings read the same. If your building has basement storage, which plenty of triple-deckers do, that's worth using for off-season bins instead of cramming everything into a closet that was never sized for a full four-season wardrobe.

We see this constantly on Boston walk-up jobs: the closet gets left for last because it looks small, and then it turns into twice as many boxes as anyone expected once every hanging item actually gets counted. Measuring the rod ahead of time, not eyeballing it, is what keeps that from happening on your move.

## The Order to Pack a Closet In

Pack in reverse order of how soon you'll need something. Off-season and rarely worn items go first, days or weeks before moving day. Formalwear, out-of-rotation shoes, and anything in the back of the closet you haven't touched in months can all go early too.

Keep a small set of daily outfits, plus whatever you're wearing on moving day itself, out until the very end. The [Boston moving day essentials box](/blog/moving-day-essentials-box-boston) is the right place for that last outfit change and a pair of shoes, not a wardrobe box that's already sealed and loaded on the truck.

## Is It Worth Paying Movers to Pack Your Closet?

Depends on the closet. A studio or one-bedroom closet with a normal amount of clothes takes an hour or two to pack yourself, and there's no real skill barrier to doing it well if you follow the rod-measurement rule above.

A walk-in with a lot of formalwear, delicate fabric, or a genuinely overwhelming volume of hanging clothes is a different story. Professional packers move faster, know how to load a wardrobe box so nothing shifts and crushes on the drive, and that packing typically falls under [your mover's valuation coverage](/blog/packing-fragile-items-boston-move) if something gets damaged, which self-packed boxes usually don't. It costs more in labor, but for a closet that would otherwise eat half a day, it's a reasonable trade, and most [local moving](/local-moving) crews can quote the packing add-on alongside the move itself instead of as a separate booking.
