Short answer: moving while pregnant is safe for most people, as long as you're not the one lifting anything. Here's the actual weight limits, the timing that matters, and what to hand off completely.

## Is It Safe to Move While Pregnant?

For an uncomplicated pregnancy, yes. The move itself isn't the hazard; a house full of boxes doesn't hurt anyone. What's risky is the bending, twisting, and repetitive heavy lifting that moving day normally involves, all of which the [American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/committee-opinion/articles/2020/04/physical-activity-and-exercise-during-pregnancy-and-the-postpartum-period) flags as something to scale back on as pregnancy progresses.

If you're managing a complication like preterm labor risk, bleeding, a multiples pregnancy, or a diagnosed condition your OB is watching, ask them directly before you commit to a move date. For everyone else, the standard advice holds: stay involved in the planning, stay out of the truck.

Talk to your doctor early regardless. Most OBs will sign off on moving with the same guidance they'd give for any physical activity — pace yourself, stay hydrated, and don't push through pain or shortness of breath. None of that requires you to skip the move. It just means someone else carries the couch.

## How Much Weight Can You Actually Lift?

Less than you'd think, and it drops as the pregnancy progresses. The CDC's [National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/reproductive-health/prevention/physical-demands.html) publishes provisional lifting limits for pregnant workers, generally in the 25-35 pound range depending on how far along you are, with the ceiling dropping further by the third trimester. NIOSH also flags lifting from below mid-shin or overhead as riskier than the weight itself, since those positions strain the lower back and core regardless of what's in your hands.

That 25-35 pound range sounds generous until you weigh an actual moving box. A box of books or dishes routinely hits 40-50 pounds. A dresser drawer left half-full because someone forgot to empty it can push past that without anyone noticing until it's already off the ground.

| Stage | General Lifting Guidance |
|---|---|
| First half of pregnancy | Up to ~35 lbs, avoid repetitive lifting |
| Second half of pregnancy | Closer to ~25 lbs, avoid bending/twisting |
| Third trimester | Lower still — no lifting, no exceptions |
| Any stage | Nothing from below mid-shin or overhead |

The practical takeaway: don't try to eyeball what's "light enough." Let the people trained to lift furniture do the lifting, and spend your energy directing rather than carrying.

## What's the Best Trimester to Move?

The second trimester, if your timeline allows it — roughly weeks 14 through 27. First-trimester fatigue and nausea have usually eased by then, and you haven't yet hit the balance shifts, swelling, and exhaustion that tend to show up in the final stretch.

Third-trimester moves aren't off the table. Plenty of people move at 32 weeks because a lease ends or a job starts and there's no other option. It just means treating the move as purely a logistics and supervision job for you, with extra breaks built in and a plan for sitting down whenever you need to.

First-trimester moves are the trickiest to plan around because symptoms are unpredictable — you might feel fine, or you might not know until the week of. If you're moving in the first trimester, build slack into the schedule and don't lock yourself into a single-day timeline with no buffer.

## Move Before the Baby Arrives, or Wait?

Before, whenever it's realistically possible. An empty or lightly-furnished home is far easier to babyproof, paint, and organize than one you're unpacking boxes in while also recovering from delivery and running on newborn sleep. Setting up a nursery in a house that's already settled beats assembling a crib around packing tape.

That said, real timelines don't always cooperate with due dates. If a lease, a job, or a sale forces the move into the weeks right after the baby arrives, don't try to run it like a normal move. Cut your to-do list down to what matters in the first 48 hours: a functional nursery, a stocked kitchen, and clear pathways. Let literally everything else sit in boxes for a week or two. Nobody remembers whether the garage got organized on schedule.

## Packing a Day-One Bag for a Newborn

If there's a baby already here by moving day, pack a separate bag that never goes near the truck. It travels in the car with you, the same way you'd handle a hospital bag.

Include diapers and wipes for at least 24 hours, formula or pumped milk if you're not exclusively nursing, two or three bottles, a couple of changes of clothes, a swaddle or sleep sack, and any prescribed medications. Add your pediatrician's phone number and a copy of the vaccination record, in case you need care before you're unpacked enough to find anything else.

Keep the crib mattress, sheets, and one familiar blanket accessible too, ideally in the same bag or a clearly labeled box loaded last so it comes off first. A baby who's overtired from a long moving day settles faster in a bed that smells and feels like home, even a partially unpacked one.

## Set Up the Nursery Before Anything Else

Once the truck is unloaded, resist the urge to unpack the kitchen first out of habit. Get the nursery functional before anything else — crib assembled, sheet on, and the space checked for the basics of [safe sleep](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/sleep/Pages/a-parents-guide-to-safe-sleep.aspx): a firm, flat sleep surface, nothing loose in the crib, and the room at a comfortable temperature.

A working nursery gives everyone, baby included, a predictable place to land while the rest of the house gets sorted over the following days. It also gives you somewhere to hand the baby off to a partner or family member while you make decisions about where the couch goes.

Newborns don't care about a fully unpacked living room. They care about a consistent feeding and sleep rhythm, and that's far easier to protect in a house where at least one room already works.

## Boston's Stairs, Parking, and Hospital-Distance Factor

Boston adds a few local wrinkles worth planning around. A huge share of the city's housing stock is [triple-deckers and walk-ups](/blog/hardest-boston-neighborhoods-to-move) with steep, narrow stairwells, exactly the kind of stairs nobody pregnant or newly postpartum should be climbing repeatedly with anything in their arms. If your current or new place is a third-floor walk-up, that's one more reason the crew, not you, makes every trip.

Parking matters more than people expect too. Narrow streets in neighborhoods like Beacon Hill, the North End, and parts of Cambridge can mean the truck parks a block away, which turns every box into a longer carry. That's the crew's problem to solve, not yours. It's still worth flagging when you book so the [move gets planned](/blog/boston-moving-day-timeline) with your building's access in mind.

If you're due close to your move date, keep your hospital's distance and route in mind when picking a moving day, and try not to schedule the move for the exact week of your due date if you can help it at all. A few days' buffer either direction removes one more variable from a day that already has enough of them.
