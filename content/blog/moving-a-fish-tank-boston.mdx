Movers will carry the tank. They won't carry the fish, and they definitely won't carry it full. Here's how to actually get an aquarium from a Boston walk-up to the next place without losing anything that's swimming in it.

## Movers Won't Take Your Fish — Here's Why

This surprises people who assume "fragile item" coverage extends to livestock. It doesn't. Every legitimate Boston mover excludes live animals from the truck [the same way they exclude plants and perishable food](/blog/what-movers-wont-move-boston), and fish sit in that category right alongside a dog or a cat. It's not a company being difficult. Standard cargo liability coverage isn't built to reimburse a living animal, and a closed truck with no climate control for several hours is a real risk to anything that needs oxygenated, temperature-stable water to survive.

The tank itself is a different story. Drained, empty, and packed correctly, it travels like any other large glass item, and a reputable crew has handled plenty of them. What has to ride with you, in your own car, is the water and everything living in it.

## How Much Water Should Actually Stay in the Tank?

None, for the tank you're loading onto the truck. This trips people up because the instinct is to leave some water in to reduce stress on the fish, but a partially full aquarium is exactly the setup that cracks a seam. Glass tanks are built to hold water evenly distributed across a stable, level surface. Tilt one with even a few inches of water sloshing inside during a ride over Boston's potholes, and you're putting uneven pressure on seals that were never designed for that kind of movement.

Drain the tank down to damp gravel before it goes anywhere. Save several gallons of the old tank water in clean buckets or a cooler instead. That water is what your fish actually travel in, separate from the tank, and it's also what keeps your gravel and filter media alive during the trip. [Fragile glass items generally travel best fully wrapped and cushioned](/blog/packing-fragile-items-boston-move), and an empty aquarium is no exception. Bubble wrap the outside, tape cardboard corners over the edges, and keep it upright the entire way. Never stack anything on top of it, even something that looks light.

## The Bag-and-Bucket Method for Your Fish

Small to medium fish travel best in sealed plastic bags, roughly one-third water to two-thirds air, tied off or clipped shut. That extra air space matters more than people expect since it's what keeps oxygen available if the trip runs long. Larger fish, or a move with more than a few individuals, generally do better in a clean five-gallon bucket with a secure, ventilated lid, since a bag that gets punctured or squeezed loses everything at once.

Whatever you use, fill it with the fish's original tank water, not fresh water from the new place. A sudden change in water chemistry is more stressful to a fish than the ride itself. Keep bags or buckets in an insulated cooler or box, and keep that cooler in the passenger area of your own car, not a trunk that can run hot in September or cold if your move lands later in the fall. Turn the car's climate control toward keeping temperature stable, not toward comfort for the humans in it.

For a local Boston move that wraps up in an afternoon, this setup holds fine. Longer moves change the math, which is the one place this guide diverges from a quick cross-town relocation.

## What About the Filter, Gravel, and Decorations?

The part people forget is that a tank isn't just glass and fish, it's also a small, established ecosystem living inside the filter. Beneficial bacteria colonize the filter media and break down waste before it becomes toxic ammonia, and that colony needs to stay wet to survive the trip. Pull the filter cartridge or sponge right before you leave and drop it into a sealed container with some of the reserved tank water. Don't let it sit out and dry on a counter while you finish loading everything else, since that's the fastest way to lose the cycle you spent weeks building.

Gravel and decorations can travel dry in a separate box, wrapped the same way you'd wrap any other fragile item, though a thin layer of tank water in a sealed bag keeps gravel bacteria alive too if your setup depends on it. Live plants inside the tank follow the same rule as houseplants elsewhere in your home: [they need to ride with you, not the truck](/blog/moving-houseplants-boston), kept damp and out of direct cold.

## How Much Does a Full Tank Actually Weigh?

This is the number that explains almost every rule in this guide. A fully set up aquarium runs close to 10 pounds per gallon once you account for the glass, substrate, and water together, per [Petco's aquarium sizing guide](https://www.petco.com/pet-education/fish/habitat-guides/aquarium-sizes-weights-and-dimensions). That weight is exactly why you drain it before moving day.

| Tank Size | Approx. Filled Weight | Approx. Empty Weight |
|---|---|---|
| 10 gallon | ~100 lbs | ~11 lbs |
| 20 gallon | ~200 lbs | ~25 lbs |
| 29 gallon | ~290 lbs | ~40 lbs |
| 40 gallon | ~400 lbs | ~50 lbs |
| 55 gallon | ~550 lbs | ~78 lbs |
| 75 gallon | ~750 lbs | ~140 lbs |

Nobody is carrying a filled 55-gallon tank down a triple-decker's back stairs, and nobody should try. Drained, that same tank drops to under 80 pounds, which is a normal two-person carry for most crews. If your building has the kind of narrow stairwell that [already causes problems for oversized furniture](/blog/furniture-wont-fit-up-boston-stairs), an empty tank is one item on the day that's actually easier than it looks on paper, as long as it's drained first.

## Boston's Tap Water Problem: Why You Can't Just Refill and Go

Here's the part that catches people off guard even when everything else about the move goes fine. Boston's water, supplied by the [MWRA](https://www.mwra.com/sites/default/files/2023-11/chloramines-qa.pdf), is treated with chloramine as a secondary disinfectant rather than plain chlorine. Chlorine alone will mostly dissipate if you let tap water sit out for a day or run an airstone through it. Chloramine doesn't. It's a more stable compound specifically because it's chlorine bonded to ammonia, and that stability is exactly why it lingers in your pipes and exactly why it won't just evaporate out of a bucket on your counter.

Refilling a drained tank with straight Boston tap water and putting your fish back in a few hours later is a fast way to undo a move that otherwise went perfectly. [Petco's dechlorination guide](https://www.petco.com/pet-education/fish/health-wellness/how-to-dechlorinate-tap-water-for-fish) is direct about the fix: use a liquid conditioner labeled for chloramine specifically, not a basic dechlorinator built only for chlorine, and treat every gallon that goes into the tank before your fish go back in, not after.

Keep a bottle of the right conditioner in your moving-day essentials bag alongside your phone charger and toothbrush. It's a five-dollar item that's easy to forget until you're standing over an empty tank at 9 p.m. with nowhere open to buy one.

## Setting Up at the New Place

Get the tank leveled and positioned first, near an outlet for the filter and heater, before you touch the water at all. Refill with properly conditioned water, let the filter and heater run for at least 20 to 30 minutes to stabilize temperature, then start bringing your fish back in.

Float their sealed bags in the newly filled tank for about 15 minutes so the temperature equalizes gradually, then open the bag and let a little tank water mix in every few minutes for another 20 to 30 minutes before releasing the fish. This slower acclimation matters more than people expect. A fish dropped straight from a bag into water that's even a few degrees off can go into shock, and that's a rough way to end a move that otherwise went smoothly. Skip the drip-and-float method entirely and you're gambling on a fish that already had a stressful day tolerating one more shock on top of it.

## Should You Just Drain It and Start Over?

For a genuinely long-distance relocation, out of state or multiple days on the road, moving live fish gets harder to justify. Extended transit means longer stretches in a bag with no filtration, bigger temperature swings, and more handoffs where something can go wrong. Some fish owners in that situation choose to rehome fish locally before an interstate move and start a new tank cycle at the destination instead, which is a legitimate call, not a failure of planning.

For anything staying within Greater Boston, though, the drain-transport-reacclimate process above works reliably. It's the same category of job as [a piano or a gun safe](/blog/moving-pianos-safes-pool-tables-boston) in one specific way: it looks intimidating mostly because it's unfamiliar, and it's actually manageable once you know the sequence.

Aquarium moves come up often enough in Boston's older walk-up buildings that most crews will ask about tank size during the pre-move walkthrough, not because anyone's planning to carry it full, but because a large glass tank changes the dolly path and stair strategy for everything else being loaded that day. Knowing about it in advance beats a crew discovering a 55-gallon tank behind a couch an hour into the job.
