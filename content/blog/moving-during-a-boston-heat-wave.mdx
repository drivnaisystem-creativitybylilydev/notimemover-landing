Boston hit its heat emergency threshold again this week, August 11 through 13. If your move date landed anywhere near it, here's what actually matters for doing it safely.

## Boston's Heat Emergencies Are Becoming a Summer Habit

This isn't a one-off. The city declared heat emergencies multiple times over the summer of 2026, including stretches in late June, early and mid-July, and now mid-August. [Boston's official threshold](https://www.boston.gov/news/mayor-wu-declares-heat-emergency-monday-august-11-through-wednesday-august-13) is a heat index of 95°F or higher for two or more consecutive days, which is exactly what triggered this week's emergency, with a forecasted heat index of 95-96°F. When that happens, the city opens BCYF cooling centers, splash pads, and misting tents at spots like City Hall Plaza.

If your move date falls inside one of these windows, don't panic and don't assume you have to cancel. Moving in the heat is manageable. It just means adjusting how you do it, not whether you do it.

## What Temperature Is Actually Too Hot to Move?

There's no single cutoff where moving becomes unsafe, but the risk curves upward fast once the heat index crosses 90°F. [OSHA's water-rest-shade guidance](https://www.osha.gov/heat-exposure/water-rest-shade) for outdoor workers is the standard reference point here: water, rest, and shade access all become more critical the higher the heat index climbs, and OSHA's proposed heat rule has been under federal review since 2024, which tells you regulators consider this a real occupational hazard, not a minor inconvenience.

For hydration specifics, [CDC/NIOSH recommends](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/heat-stress/recommendations/) about one cup of water every 15-20 minutes during sustained physical activity in the heat, and switching to an electrolyte drink if sweating continues for several hours. That applies to you carrying boxes down a stairwell just as much as it applies to the crew.

The practical line most movers use: below 90°F, proceed normally with extra water breaks. Above 95°F, expect a slower pace and more frequent breaks. Above 100°F heat index, it's worth talking to your mover about shifting the start time or the date entirely.

## Protect Electronics and Other Heat-Sensitive Items First

A closed moving truck sitting in direct sun can climb well past 120°F inside, even with outside temps in the 90s. That's brutal on anything with a battery or a screen.

| Item | Heat Risk | Where It Should Ride |
|---|---|---|
| Laptops, TVs, game consoles | Battery and screen damage above 100°F | Air-conditioned car |
| Vinyl records, candles | Warping and melting | Air-conditioned car |
| Houseplants | Wilting, shock, sometimes death | Your car, never the truck |
| Medications | Degraded effectiveness | With you, climate-controlled |
| Wood furniture, mattresses | Minor risk, mostly fine | Truck is fine |

[Houseplants](/blog/moving-houseplants-boston) already can't ride in a dark, unclimatized cargo hold under normal conditions, and summer heat makes that worse, not better. The same logic applies to anything electronic: give it the air-conditioned seat, not the truck.

Let anything that did spend time in the heat cool down slowly before you plug it back in. A laptop that's been sitting at 110°F for two hours needs to reach room temperature before you power it on, or you risk condensation shorting something out.

## Should You Reschedule for a Heat Advisory?

Usually not, but it depends entirely on what you'd be trading. A move already scheduled for a day with a 95°F+ heat index doesn't need to be scrapped. It needs an earlier start and realistic expectations about pace.

Where rescheduling makes sense: extreme heat forecasts above 100°F, a household member with heat sensitivity, or a walk-up move with multiple flights and no realistic way to shorten the carry distance. Where it doesn't: a standard local move where you can just start two hours earlier and add water breaks.

The deciding factor is usually your pricing structure. If your rate is locked in regardless of date, moving your date costs you nothing but a phone call. If you booked an hourly quote tied to a specific slot, rescheduling might mean losing your spot in a packed summer schedule. Ask this before you book, not on the morning of a heat emergency.

## Schedule an Early Start, Not a Long Day

The single biggest lever you control is start time. Boston's heat index [typically peaks between 2 and 6 p.m.](/blog/boston-moving-day-timeline), so a crew loaded and rolling by 8 a.m. does the heaviest lifting before the day gets dangerous.

An early start also means more slack if something runs long, a jammed elevator, a narrow stairwell, a truck that can't park close. On a normal day that's an inconvenience. On a 95°F afternoon, it's the difference between finishing in daylight comfort and finishing exhausted at 4 p.m.

If you're booking during a heat wave, ask directly for the earliest available slot. Most crews would rather start at 7 a.m. in August than 11 a.m., for the same reasons you would.

## Are Movers Required to Take Breaks in Extreme Heat?

There's no single federal law mandating specific break intervals for movers yet, and Massachusetts' current heat standard covers public-sector outdoor workers, not private moving crews. What exists right now is guidance, not a hard requirement, which means the actual practice comes down to how a company runs its crews.

Here's a general pattern worth knowing: Boston jobs in July and August routinely involve carrying furniture through un-air-conditioned stairwells and hallways, often on the exact days the crew would most want a break. A crew that builds water and shade time into a hot job isn't being inefficient. They're avoiding heat exhaustion on a job that already involves heavy lifting on stairs.

## Triple-Deckers and Walk-Ups Make Boston Heat Worse

Boston's housing stock works against you on a hot day. A huge share of the city's [hardest neighborhoods to move in and out of](/blog/hardest-boston-neighborhoods-to-move) are triple-deckers and walk-ups with no elevator and, often, no central air on the upper floors. Top-floor units in Somerville, Allston, and Dorchester routinely run hotter than the outside air by the time you're moving out in August.

Narrow streets compound it. If the truck can't park directly in front, every box means a longer walk in direct sun instead of a quick trip from curb to door. That's exactly the kind of detail that turns a manageable 90°F move into an exhausting one.

If you know your building fits this description, budget extra time and extra water, and don't schedule back-to-back appointments (utility hookups, cleaning crews, key handoffs) immediately after your load-out. Heat noticeably slows a move with stairs and long carries, and pretending otherwise just means you're rushing the crew through the hottest part of the day.
