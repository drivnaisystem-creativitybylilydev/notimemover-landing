Heaviest items go in first, against the front wall, with roughly 60% of your total weight sitting in the front half of the truck. Get that backwards and you'll find out on the highway, not in the driveway, when a $40 lamp becomes a cracked windshield somewhere on 93.

## How to Load a Moving Truck: Heavy Items Go First

Trucks are built nose-heavy on purpose. The cab sits over or ahead of the front axle, and the whole vehicle handles best when the cargo weight matches that bias. [U-Haul's own truck-loading instructions](https://www.uhaul.com/Tips/Loading/Truck-User-Instructions-123/) confirm the same target: refrigerators, washers, dryers, dressers, and any solid wood furniture go in first, against the front wall of the cargo box, on the floor, before anything else touches the truck.

Load the light stuff first and stack the heavy pieces toward the rear door instead, and the truck starts to sway in crosswinds and takes longer to stop. On the Pike or crossing the Tobin, that's not a small thing. It's also the single most common mistake on a first-time DIY load, because boxes are easier to grab than a dresser, so they tend to go in first out of habit rather than plan.

## How Much Truck Do You Actually Need?

Plan on roughly 150-200 cubic feet of truck space per fully furnished room, then book 10-15% more than your estimate. In a city where you rarely get a convenient second trip, that buffer matters more than it sounds like it should.

| Truck Size | Cargo Space | Good For |
|---|---|---|
| Cargo van | ~400 cu ft | Studio |
| 12-ft truck | ~450 cu ft | Dorm or small 1BR |
| 16-ft truck | ~800 cu ft | 1-2BR apartment |
| 22-ft truck | ~1,200 cu ft | 2-3BR |
| 26-ft truck | ~1,700 cu ft | 4+ BR house |

Sizing comes from [Penske's own truck size guide](https://www.pensketruckrental.com/knowledge-center/moving-truck-sizes/), and it lines up with what most rental chains publish. Undersizing is the more common mistake. A truck that's slightly too big costs a little more in rental fees; a truck that's too small means a second trip, a storage unit run, or items strapped to a roof rack that shouldn't be.

## What's the Actual Loading Order?

Think in three sections, built front to back.

The base is your heaviest, bulkiest pieces stacked floor to ceiling against the front wall, wrapped in furniture pads so nothing scrapes. This is the fridge, the dresser, the dining table on its side.

The middle is where furniture doubles as a load-bearing wall. Stand mattresses and box springs on their sides to create a vertical divider, then fill in boxes behind and around them, heaviest boxes on the bottom of each stack, lightest on top. Strap off each finished tier before starting the next one, rather than building the whole load and strapping it once at the end.

The rear, closest to the door, holds whatever you need first at the new place and anything genuinely fragile. It's the last thing loaded and the first thing off, which also means it takes the least jostling over the course of the drive.

## Why a Triple-Decker Move Changes the Math

Boston's housing stock makes this harder than the national loading guides assume. A three-flight walk-up in Somerville or Jamaica Plain means every box already made a trip down a narrow stairwell before it reaches the truck. Crews are working with tired arms and awkward angles well before loading even starts, so a clean, efficient loading order matters more here than in a house with a driveway and a ground-floor door.

Parking adds a second constraint. A [moving truck parking permit](/blog/boston-moving-truck-parking-permit) reserves your spot for a fixed window, not an open-ended one, and narrow streets in the North End or Beacon Hill often mean there's exactly one place the truck can legally sit. You don't always get to circle back and reload if the first attempt goes sideways. That's a real argument for getting the order right the first time, especially if your move date lands anywhere near September 1, when trucks are running back-to-back jobs across the city with no slack in the schedule.

## Should You Disassemble Furniture Before Loading?

For anything large, yes. [Bed frames, large sectionals, and table legs](/blog/furniture-disassembly-boston-move) take up dramatically less cargo space broken down, and they're far less likely to gouge a stairwell wall or catch on a doorframe on the way out. A queen bed frame loaded whole can eat as much truck space as four or five medium boxes.

It also makes the loading order easier to keep even. Flat-packed furniture pieces slide into gaps between bigger items instead of forcing you to leave awkward air pockets that nothing else fits into cleanly.

## How Do You Keep the Load From Shifting?

Straps do the real work here, not just tape or rope looped once around a stack. Run a strap across each tier of boxes before you build the next one, anchored to the truck's built-in rail system if it has one. Moving blankets go between furniture pieces, not just around the outside of the load, since two wood surfaces rubbing together for three hours will scratch.

Fill gaps with soft, compressible items, pillows, rolled rugs, folded blankets, instead of leaving open space. An empty pocket in the load is exactly where things start to move once the truck takes its first hard turn.

## The Loading Mistakes That Cost People Their Deposit (or Their Stuff)

A few patterns show up over and over on DIY loads:

- Loading the lightest, easiest-to-grab boxes first because it feels like progress, then running out of room for the heavy furniture and forcing it in wherever it fits. That's the loading order in reverse, and it's the single biggest cause of a rough drive.
- Piling all the heavy items against one wall of the truck instead of balancing left to right. A truck that's heavier on one side leans and handles poorly, even if the front-to-back weight is correct.
- Skipping the tape measure. A couch that clears your apartment door might not clear the stairwell turn, and finding that out with the truck already loaded costs real time.
- Leaving the securing straps for the very end instead of tying off each section as you go, which is how a stable-looking load turns into a shifted mess halfway through the drive.

## DIY Loading vs. Letting a Crew Handle It

Renting a truck looks like the cheap option until you price in the actual labor of building a load correctly. Most people load a truck exactly once or twice in their life. A professional crew does it several times a week, which is the entire reason [DIY moves and hired moves land at such different price points](/blog/diy-move-vs-hiring-movers-boston) once you account for time, rental fees, and the risk of a bad load.

There's also a deposit angle nobody mentions until it's too late. Most truck rental contracts hold you responsible for interior damage, and a load that shifts hard enough to dent a wall or crack a piece of furniture on the way out is coming out of that deposit either way. A crew that loads trucks for a living isn't guessing at the weight distribution; it's routine.
