Moving in together isn't a bigger version of a normal move. It's two households becoming one, and that's a genuinely different problem.

## Why Moving In Together Isn't Just a Bigger Move

Most people plan a move-in-together the same way they'd plan any other move: book a truck, pack some boxes, done. Then the truck shows up and there are two couches, two full sets of dishes, and a new apartment that only has room for one of each.

This is common enough that [Pew Research](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/08/12/most-us-adults-have-lived-with-a-romantic-partner-theyre-not-married-to-by-their-early-40s/) found that about three-quarters of adults in their late 30s and early 40s have lived with an unmarried romantic partner at some point. Moving in together is one of the most common moves people make, and yet almost nobody plans for the part that actually makes it hard: merging two functioning households into one.

The truck and the boxes are the easy part. Deciding whose stuff survives the move, whose lease gets broken, and where the extra furniture goes is the part worth planning for first.

## Talk Money and Space Before You Talk Boxes

Have two conversations before you book anything: money and space.

On money, most couples land on one of two models. An even 50/50 split works when incomes are close. A proportional split, where each person pays a percentage of shared costs based on what they earn, tends to work better when incomes differ. Either way, decide who's paying for the movers, the boxes, and the first month's shared expenses before the bill shows up, not after.

On space, be honest about what you're actually working with. A Boston studio or one-bedroom rarely has the closet space, kitchen cabinets, or floor area to comfortably hold two fully-furnished apartments' worth of belongings. Walk the new place with a tape measure before you assume anything will fit. That conversation is a lot easier in your own kitchen than it is on move day with a couch stuck in the doorway.

## What Do You Do With All the Duplicates?

Every couple merging households runs into the same categories of overlap. Decide on a rule for each one instead of relitigating it item by item.

| Category | Typical Duplicates | Decision Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Kitchen | Pots, pans, dishes, small appliances | Keep the better-condition set, sell or donate the other |
| Living room furniture | Two couches, two coffee tables | Measure the new space first, keep whatever actually fits |
| Bedroom | Two beds, two dressers | Keep the better mattress, repurpose or sell the spare frame |
| Electronics | Two TVs, two routers | Keep the newer one, sell or gift the other |
| Sentimental items | Family furniture, gifts, keepsakes | If neither of you will give it up, store it short-term and decide later |

On Boston jobs, this is where crews watch things stall out. Two couches show up at the truck, a full apartment's worth of dishes gets boxed, and only then does someone measure the new kitchen cabinets. Sort the duplicates before the truck arrives, not while it's parked outside on a meter. If you need help getting rid of what doesn't make the cut, [our decluttering guide](/blog/declutter-before-a-boston-move) covers where in Boston to actually offload it fast.

## Should You Break Your Lease, Sublet It, or Let It Run Out?

This is the question couples skip until it's urgent. In Massachusetts, moving in with a partner generally isn't, on its own, a legal reason for a landlord to let you out of a lease early. If your lease still has months left, you've usually got three options: keep paying rent on an apartment you're not living in, sublet it, or negotiate a lease-break directly with your landlord.

Subletting is the most common workaround, but it comes with a catch. Per [MassLegalHelp's tenant rights guide](https://www.masslegalhelp.org/housing-apartments-shelter/tenants-rights/subletting-your-apartment), you need your landlord's written permission unless your lease specifically allows subletting without it, and you stay legally responsible for the apartment even after a subtenant moves in. If they stop paying rent, your landlord can still come after you.

If your lease happens to run through [September 1](/blog/moving-september-1-boston), Boston's mass lease-turnover date, that timing can actually work in your favor. Let it expire naturally instead of trying to break it early, and you'll avoid the sublet paperwork entirely, even if it means paying rent on an empty apartment for a few extra weeks.

## Measure Before You Fall in Love With a Floor Plan

A floor plan that looks fine on paper falls apart fast in an actual Boston walk-up. Narrow stairwells, tight turns on triple-decker landings, and doorways that were never sized for a modern sectional are the norm, not the exception, in a lot of the city's older housing stock.

Before you decide which couch, dresser, or dining table survives the merge, measure two things: the piece itself, and the path it has to travel to get into the new place. A couch that's perfect for the new living room is a problem if it can't clear the stairwell. Some pieces need to come apart to make the trip; our [furniture disassembly guide](/blog/furniture-disassembly-boston-move) covers what typically needs to be broken down for a tight Boston move and what doesn't.

## One Moving Truck or Two?

If both leases end around the same date, one shared booking with two pickup stops is usually cheaper and a lot less chaotic than booking two separate movers. You're paying for one crew and one truck route instead of two minimum-hour bookings, and you only have to coordinate with one company instead of two schedules.

The cost-splitting logic here works a lot like it does for [roommates splitting a shared move](/blog/moving-with-roommates-boston): an even split if you're bringing similar amounts of stuff, or a weighted split if one of you is moving a fully-furnished apartment and the other is moving three boxes and a bike. Agree on the number before you book, not after.

Two separate bookings make more sense when your move-out dates are weeks apart, or when one of you is coming from outside Boston. A [local move](/local-moving) with a locked-in quote removes at least one variable from a day that already has enough moving pieces.

## Where Does the Extra Stuff Go in the Meantime?

Not every decision needs to get made on move day. If you're not ready to part with a piece of furniture, or your two move-out dates don't line up cleanly, short-term storage is the reasonable middle ground.

This comes up more than people expect, especially when one lease runs longer than the other. Rather than cramming everything into an apartment that isn't ready for it, a month or two of storage buys time to actually live in the new place before deciding what stays. Our [storage options guide](/blog/boston-storage-options-during-a-move) breaks down what's realistic for a city without much driveway space to work with.

## Make the New Place Feel Like Both of Yours

Once the boxes are in, set up the bedroom and kitchen first. Those two rooms make an apartment livable fast, and everything else can wait a few days without anyone feeling like they're camping.

Even in a small Boston apartment, carve out one thing that's unmistakably each person's, a reading chair, a side of the closet, a corner of the desk. It sounds minor next to the lease paperwork and the furniture math, but it's usually what makes a merged apartment actually feel shared instead of like one person moved into the other's place.
