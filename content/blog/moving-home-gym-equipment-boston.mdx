Short answer: a Peloton Bike runs about 135 lbs and a Tread nearly 300, roughly the weight of an adult male deer and a small motorcycle. Here's how to get a Boston home gym from one apartment to the next without a cracked floor, a busted treadmill deck, or a surprise fee on move day.

## How Much Does Home Gym Equipment Actually Weigh?

Home gyms got a lot more common in Boston apartments over the last several years, and movers have adjusted to seeing a Peloton or a rack in the corner of what used to be a spare bedroom. The weights matter more than most people assume before they've tried to carry one down three flights.

Here's the baseline, using manufacturer specs where they exist rather than guesses.

| Equipment | Approximate Weight | Move Difficulty |
|---|---|---|
| Peloton Bike / Bike+ | 135-140 lbs | Moderate: awkward shape, one delicate screen |
| Treadmill (folding) | 200-250 lbs | Moderate: heavy but mobile on wheels |
| Peloton Tread | ~290 lbs | High: no folding, needs a real crew |
| Squat rack (empty) | 80-150 lbs | Moderate: bulky, needs disassembly |
| Olympic barbell + full plate set | 300-500 lbs total | High: heaviest by total pounds, splits into small units |
| Adjustable dumbbells (pair) | 50-100 lbs | Low: heavy but boxable |

[Peloton's own support documentation](https://support.onepeloton.com/hc/en-us/articles/4414302584724-Peloton-Tread-and-Tread-Dimensions-and-Specifications) confirms the Tread's weight and dimensions directly, which is worth checking against your specific model before booking movers, since Peloton has updated hardware more than once.

## Will Your Floor Actually Hold It?

Probably, but not always in the spot you'd assume. Residential construction in the U.S. is generally built to the [International Residential Code's live load standard](https://codes.iccsafe.org/s/IRC2021P2/chapter-3-building-planning/IRC2021P2-Pt03-Ch03-SecR301.5) of 40 pounds per square foot for habitable rooms, which is the same code baseline used for a room full of furniture and people. Spread a 400-pound rack setup across six or eight square feet of floor and you're nowhere near that limit.

The real risk in Boston's older triple-deckers and pre-war walk-ups isn't total weight, it's concentration. A squat rack with four narrow feet, or a stack of plates piled in one corner instead of spread across a shelf, puts real pressure on a small patch of century-old floorboards. Ground floors over a finished basement are usually fine. A third-floor unit in a building that hasn't been renovated since the 1920s is where it's worth asking your landlord or a contractor before you set up permanently, not just before you move.

None of this is a reason to panic about moving day itself. The floor concern is about where equipment lives for months or years, not about a crew carrying it through for twenty minutes.

## Disassembling a Peloton Bike or Tread

Power everything off and unplug it first, always. The touchscreen is the most fragile part of either machine and the first thing that should come off, wrapped separately in a thick blanket or its own box rather than left attached to the frame during transport.

On the Bike, the pedals unscrew with an Allen wrench, the left one clockwise and the right one counterclockwise, and the weight holder and bottle cage come off with a few more bolts. On the Tread, there's generally less to disassemble since the frame doesn't fold, which is exactly why it needs a full crew and a clear path rather than a single mover wheeling it solo.

[U-Pack's exercise equipment moving guide](https://www.upack.com/articles/how-to-move-exercise-equipment) walks through the same basic order for other cardio machines: screen or console off first, small parts bagged and labeled, frame wrapped last. That sequence holds regardless of brand.

## Treadmills: Fold It, Don't Force It

If your treadmill folds, lock the deck upright before movers arrive and leave it there. That single step turns a floor-length machine into something roughly the size of a large suitcase, which makes the difference between an easy carry and a doorway standoff.

Non-folding decks are a different job. Those typically need the console removed and sometimes the deck separated from the base to clear a standard 30-32 inch doorway, let alone a narrow stairwell in a Somerville triple-decker. Always remove the safety key and tape it somewhere secure on the frame. A treadmill that gets bumped and thinks someone just started running is a genuinely bad surprise on a loading dock.

## How Do You Pack Weight Racks, Plates, and Dumbbells?

Split it up. Plates, dumbbells, and kettlebells belong in small, heavy-duty boxes packed no more than half full, never in one giant box that two people can't lift without a hernia. Movers who show up to a single 40-pound box labeled "misc" and find 150 pounds of iron plates inside are, reasonably, going to be annoyed and slower for the rest of the job.

Racks and benches disassemble with a standard Allen key or socket set in most cases, following whatever manual came in the box or is posted on the manufacturer's site if you've lost the paper copy. Wrap uprights and crossbars in moving blankets and secure them with stretch wrap so bolts don't scratch other furniture in the truck. If your rack weighs north of 300 pounds assembled or has a complicated cable pulley system, that's the same call as any other bulky furniture: our [furniture disassembly guide](/blog/furniture-disassembly-boston-move) covers when it's worth letting the crew handle it instead of doing it yourself the morning of.

## How Much Will Movers Charge for Gym Equipment?

Expect a specialty-item fee once a single piece crosses roughly 100 to 150 pounds or needs three people instead of two, priced similarly to how [pianos, safes, and pool tables](/blog/moving-pianos-safes-pool-tables-boston) get billed. It's rarely folded quietly into the hourly rate. Stairs push the number up fast, same as with any heavy item — a Tread coming down two flights in a walk-up costs more than the identical Tread rolling out a ground-floor slider door.

The fix is the same one that applies to every heavy or awkward item: tell your mover what you own before move day, not when the truck is already parked. A pricing model that locks your total in upfront only works if the quote reflects what's actually getting carried, which is exactly what a [local move](/local-moving) quote should account for from the start. We've watched plenty of Boston jobs get more expensive at the door specifically because a squat rack or a full-size Tread never made it into the original conversation, and that's an entirely avoidable cost.

## Elevators, Stairs, and Boston Walk-Ups

Freight elevators in Boston's managed high-rises can typically handle far more weight than a home gym ever will, so capacity isn't usually the blocker. What is a blocker: reservation timing and the [Certificate of Insurance most buildings require](/blog/boston-high-rise-moving-elevator-coi) before your movers can even touch the elevator. Book both well ahead of move day if you live in one of the Seaport or Back Bay towers with a doorman and a property manager.

Walk-ups are the harder case, and they're the default in a lot of Boston neighborhoods. A non-folding Tread down three flights of a narrow Allston staircase with a tight turn at the landing is a two- or three-person job every time, no exceptions. Measure your stairwell width and any turns before move day so your mover can plan the right crew size instead of discovering the problem with the machine halfway down.

## Should You Just Sell It and Rebuy?

Worth doing the math before you assume moving is automatically the right call. A specialty-item fee on a heavy piece can run $100-$300 depending on stairs and distance, on top of your own time disassembling and reassembling it. For a $150 basic weight bench or a rack you bought secondhand for cheap, selling locally and buying another one at the new place is often the more rational move financially.

That math flips for anything with real resale value. A Peloton retains a meaningful chunk of its original price on the used market, and a barbell with a real set of cast-iron plates is expensive to replace new. Those are worth the disassembly and the fee. A wobbly $80 folding bench from a big-box store generally isn't.
