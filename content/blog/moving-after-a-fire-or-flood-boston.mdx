Short answer: you can usually start moving belongings out within 24-48 hours of a fire or flood, once the building is cleared safe. What comes after that is the part most moving guides skip entirely.

## Moving After a Fire vs. a Burst Pipe

In Boston, this almost always comes down to one of two things: a fire, or a burst pipe during a hard freeze. Fires are less frequent but more destructive. Frozen pipes are far more common, especially in triple-deckers and older buildings with unheated basements, exposed exterior-wall plumbing, or units that sit empty over a long weekend with the heat turned down.

Water damage and freezing account for roughly a quarter of all homeowners insurance claims nationally, second only to wind and hail, according to the [Insurance Information Institute](https://www.iii.org/fact-statistic/facts-statistics-homeowners-and-renters-insurance). Fire is rarer but far more damaging per incident. The [National Fire Protection Association](https://www.nfpa.org/education-and-research/research/nfpa-research/fire-statistical-reports/home-structure-fires) recorded roughly 330,000 home structure fires nationwide in 2024 between one- and two-family homes and apartments. Either one can leave you needing to move belongings out within a day, not a month.

## What Do You Do in the First 24 Hours?

Get out safely, then get confirmation before you go back in. A fire department needs to clear a fire scene as structurally safe before anyone re-enters; in a flood, that confirmation might come from a building manager or the city instead. Trying to grab things during active smoke conditions or standing water isn't worth the risk, even for something valuable.

Once you're cleared to re-enter, grab documents and irreplaceables before anything else: passports, birth certificates, insurance paperwork, prescriptions, and whatever has real sentimental or resale value. Everything else can wait until the space is fully assessed. Then call your insurer immediately, even before you know the full scope of damage. Most policies require prompt notice, and the sooner an adjuster is assigned, the sooner temporary housing costs start getting reimbursed. If you rent, call your landlord and your renters insurance provider the same day; Massachusetts landlords generally aren't on the hook for your personal property, only the building itself.

A word on safety, since people skip this in the rush to salvage things: if a fire marshal or building inspector hasn't formally cleared the unit, don't go back in for "just a few minutes" to grab something. Structural damage isn't always visible, and smoke or standing water can hide compromised flooring, live wiring, or mold that's already started growing behind drywall.

## Document Everything Before You Touch Anything

This is the step people skip because they're in a hurry to clean up, and it's the one that costs the most later. Photograph and video every room before anything gets moved, thrown out, or piled by the curb. Wide shots of each room, then close-ups of specific damage: scorch marks, water lines on drywall, warped flooring, ruined furniture.

If you already have a [home inventory](/blog/home-inventory-checklist-boston-move) from before the loss, this is exactly what it's for. If you don't, reconstruct one now from receipts, photos on your phone, and old delivery emails. Adjusters work off documentation, not memory, and a claim with thorough photos moves faster than one built on a homeowner's best recollection weeks later.

## Does Homeowners Insurance Cover the Move?

Usually, if the underlying damage is a covered peril. Most standard homeowners and renters policies include Additional Living Expenses, sometimes called loss-of-use coverage, which pays for temporary housing, storage, and reasonable moving costs while your home is uninhabitable. Coverage typically runs until repairs are done, capped at a percentage of your dwelling limit, so a bigger loss with a longer rebuild timeline eats into that cap faster.

| Loss Type | Typical Move-Out Window | What ALE Usually Covers |
|---|---|---|
| Minor flood, one or two rooms | Days to a couple weeks | Hotel or short-term rental, cleanup-related moving costs |
| Major flood or burst pipe, multiple floors | Several weeks to a few months | Temporary housing, storage, moving both directions |
| Structural fire damage | Months, sometimes longer | Temporary housing, full-home storage, moving costs both ways |

Keep every receipt tied to the move itself. ALE reimbursement depends on proof of expense, not an estimate.

## Where Do Your Belongings Go During Restoration?

Anything salvageable needs to go somewhere while contractors gut and rebuild, and that's rarely your existing unit. For a smaller loss, a [storage unit](/blog/boston-storage-options-during-a-move) close to the property works fine. For a full-home fire, a pack-out into long-term storage is standard, sometimes with a separate cleaning company handling smoke or soot remediation on textiles before anything gets stored.

Ask your insurer whether they're covering "contents pack-out" as part of the claim; many policies do, and it can offset what you'd otherwise pay a moving company directly. Get that answer before you book anything, since it changes who's paying the bill.

In a city built on triple-deckers and converted brownstones, this step gets complicated fast. A single burst pipe on a third floor can send water down through two occupied units below it, which means three households, three claims, and three separate move-out timelines running at once in the same building. Coordinate with your building's other tenants or owners early if that's your situation, since the restoration crew usually needs all three units cleared before work can start on any of them.

## What to Salvage and What to Let Go

Don't make this call yourself if there's an insurance claim involved. Photograph items in place, then let the adjuster or a public adjuster assess what's salvageable before anything goes in the dumpster. Porous materials that absorbed smoke or contaminated water (mattresses, upholstered furniture, carpet, most soft goods) usually don't survive cleaning even when they look fine on the surface.

Hard goods fare better. Electronics, metal furniture, and sealed containers often just need cleaning, though anything electronic that took on water should be checked by a professional before it's powered back on. The [Red Cross's post-fire guidance](https://www.redcross.org/get-help/how-to-prepare-for-emergencies/types-of-emergencies/fire/home-fire-recovery.html) is worth reading in full here, since it covers what's safe to keep beyond just the insurance angle, including food, medication, and cosmetics that were exposed to heat or smoke.

You don't necessarily need a public adjuster to get through this. For a single flooded room or a contained kitchen fire, most homeowners deal directly with their insurer's adjuster without issue. A public adjuster, who works for you rather than the insurance company, tends to earn their fee on the bigger losses: a full-floor fire, a multi-unit flood, or any claim where the insurer's first offer feels low relative to what restoration contractors are actually quoting.

## Two Claims, Not One: Movers vs. Homeowners Insurance

People conflate these, and they're separate processes. Your homeowners or renters claim covers the fire or flood damage itself and the ALE costs tied to displacement. A [moving company's damage claim](/blog/moving-damage-claim-boston) only covers something the movers themselves damaged or lost during the move, which is a much narrower, separate claim filed with the mover directly, not your insurer.

Keep the two straight in your paperwork. If a mover drops a box during the emergency move and something inside breaks, that's a claim against the moving company under its own liability coverage, filed on its own timeline, regardless of how the underlying fire or flood claim is proceeding with your insurer.
