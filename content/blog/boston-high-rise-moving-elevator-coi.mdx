Book the freight elevator two to four weeks out and get your mover's Certificate of Insurance on file before that, or the front desk turns your crew away at the door. That's the short version. Here's the exact paperwork and timeline Boston high-rises expect.

## The Two Documents That Gate Every High-Rise Move

Managed buildings (condos, doorman buildings, larger apartment complexes with on-site staff) almost universally require two things before they'll let a moving truck near the loading dock: a booked freight elevator reservation and a Certificate of Insurance (COI) from your mover. Skip either one and building staff can, and often will, turn your crew away at the door. Boston has a lot of these buildings now, concentrated in the Seaport, Back Bay, the West End, and the Downtown/Financial District corridor, and they all run some version of the same two-document check.

A COI is a one-page document your moving company's insurer issues, confirming they carry active general liability coverage and, usually, naming the building as an "additional insured" for your specific move date. It's not the same thing as [moving insurance for your belongings](/blog/moving-insurance-boston). That's a separate coverage that protects your stuff if something breaks. A COI protects the building's lobby, elevator interior, and hallways from damage during the move itself. Getting that document in order is the building's problem to solve, not yours, which is exactly why they ask for it before anyone touches the freight elevator.

This is also a reasonable proxy for whether a mover is legitimate in the first place. The [American Moving and Storage Association](https://www.moving.org) advises confirming a mover's insurance and registration before you book, and a company that can't produce a COI on request for a [local move](/local-moving) is telling you something about how they operate generally, not just about this one building's paperwork.

## What Coverage Do Boston Buildings Actually Ask For?

The number that shows up most often is $1,000,000 in general liability coverage, according to [SQ Moving's COI guide](https://sqmoving.com/certificate-of-insurance-for-moving/), which is fairly standard across the moving industry nationally, not just in Boston. Luxury buildings sometimes ask for more.

| Building Type | Typical GL Minimum | Additional Insured Required? |
|---|---|---|
| Standard apartment building, on-site manager | $1,000,000 | Usually |
| Condo association | $1,000,000 | Almost always |
| Luxury or doorman high-rise | $1,000,000-$2,000,000 | Always |

Any legitimate, [fully insured moving company](/blog/what-fully-insured-means-boston-movers) should clear the standard $1M threshold without any special arrangement. This is baseline coverage, not an upgrade. Where people get stuck isn't the coverage amount. It's the exact wording buildings want: the precise legal name and address for the "certificate holder," sometimes a management company name that's different from the building's name on the sign out front, and a hard deadline for when it needs to be on file. Get that wording wrong and the building can bounce the certificate back and ask for a corrected version, which burns days you don't have if you waited until the week of your move.

## Booking the Freight Elevator

Reservations typically open two to four weeks out, and slots run in narrow windows: two to four hours, usually weekday business hours, sometimes with weekend slots at a premium or blocked off entirely. Book too late and you're choosing from whatever's left, which near the end of a month can mean nothing at all.

Two windows on the calendar make this worse. September 1st is [Boston's biggest lease-turnover day](/blog/moving-september-1-boston), and buildings with any meaningful unit count are booked solid weeks out. The last three or four days of any month run a smaller version of the same problem, since a chunk of leases turn over on the first regardless of season.

Call or email the property manager as soon as you have a move date, not after you've booked movers. Ask for the reservation form, the exact COI requirements, and whether there's a separate damage deposit. Many buildings collect one, commonly somewhere in the $150-$500 range, refundable once a staff member confirms the elevator and hallways came through the move undamaged. That deposit has nothing to do with your moving company's insurance or your quote; it's strictly between you and the building. Getting this whole list on day one saves you from finding out about a missing requirement 48 hours before your move.

## What If You Miss the Window?

Building staff generally won't bend on this. Show up without a reservation, or outside your booked block, and the front desk turns your crew away — no elevator access, no exceptions, regardless of how far your movers drove to get there. You're now rescheduling with the building, rebooking your movers around whatever their calendar allows, and possibly eating a trip fee for the crew's wasted morning.

The fix is boring but works: confirm your reservation 48 hours out, and confirm your COI was actually received and accepted, not just sent. Property managers are juggling a lot of buildings and a lot of email. A document sitting unread in an inbox is functionally the same as never having sent it.

Some buildings will let you swap into a same-day cancellation if one opens up, but that's luck, not a plan. Treat the 48-hour confirmation call as mandatory, even when everything looks fine on paper. It's a five-minute call that catches the rare case where a form got filed under the wrong unit number or a coverage minimum changed since you last checked.

## Triple-Deckers and Walk-Ups Skip This Entirely

Here's the split that surprises people new to Boston: none of this applies if you're moving into a triple-decker, a small walk-up, or most three-to-six-unit buildings without an on-site manager. There's no freight elevator to reserve and usually no property management company to hand a COI to. The tradeoff is stairs, not paperwork, and [tipping crews more](/blog/how-much-to-tip-movers-boston) tends to reflect that.

If you're weighing a [walk-up in Jamaica Plain or Somerville against a managed building in the Seaport or Back Bay](/blog/hardest-boston-neighborhoods-to-move), factor this into the decision. A high-rise move is often smoother on moving day itself, once the paperwork clears. A walk-up move has zero paperwork but more physical labor and a less predictable timeline if your building's one elevator (if it has one at all) happens to be out of service.

Smaller three-family and four-family buildings sometimes have an owner who lives on site instead of a management company. If that's your situation, it's worth a quick call anyway. Some owner-occupied buildings still want a COI for their own peace of mind, even without a formal policy requiring one, especially if they've had damage from a move before.

## Who Actually Handles the COI: You or the Mover?

The mover generates the document. You're the one who has to relay the building's specific requirements to them early enough to matter. That means getting the reservation form and insurance requirements from your property manager, then forwarding the coverage minimums, the exact certificate holder name, and the deadline to your movers the same week you book them, not the week of your move. Movers who do a lot of high-rise work will usually ask for this paperwork upfront, but plenty won't think to unless you bring it up first, so don't assume it's automatically handled.

We build this into the quote process for any high-rise job, because it's a predictable ask, not a surprise. Crews already carry coverage that clears the standard $1M threshold, so turning around a COI is usually a same-day request once we have the building's exact wording, not something that holds up your reservation.

## A Realistic Timeline

| Timing | Task |
|---|---|
| 4-6 weeks out | Confirm move date, contact property management for reservation form and COI requirements |
| 3-4 weeks out | Book your movers, forward building requirements to them |
| 2-3 weeks out | Book the freight elevator reservation |
| 1-2 weeks out | Confirm COI was submitted and accepted (not just sent) |
| 48 hours out | Confirm the elevator reservation directly with the front desk or property manager |

This runs longer than [the general booking window for Boston movers](/blog/how-far-in-advance-to-book-movers-boston), and that's the point: a high-rise move has an extra layer of building bureaucracy stacked on top of the normal moving logistics, and none of it moves faster just because your moving date is fixed.

## What to Ask Your Property Manager Before You Book

A short list, but missing any one of these is what causes last-minute scrambles:

- Exact certificate holder name and address for the COI (often the management company, not the building)
- Minimum general liability coverage required, and whether workers' comp or umbrella coverage is also needed
- How far in advance the COI needs to be received, not just sent
- Elevator reservation length and available time blocks
- Whether there's a separate refundable damage deposit, and how it's collected
- Truck size restrictions or designated loading zones outside the building
