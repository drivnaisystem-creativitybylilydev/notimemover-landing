Short answer: a 10-foot truck for a studio, up to a 26-footer for a 3-bedroom. In Boston, though, whether that truck can actually reach your door matters just as much as its size. Here's how to get both parts right.

## The Boston-Specific Twist: Volume vs. Access

Every national truck-size guide treats this as a one-variable problem: how much stuff do you have, pick the truck that fits it. Boston makes it a two-variable problem. The truck also has to fit the street.

A 26-foot truck is the right size on paper for a lot of 3-bedroom moves. It's also too long to turn onto plenty of side streets in the North End, too tall for a few low-hanging branches and awnings in Jamaica Plain, and a genuine headache to parallel park on a one-way in Southie. Meanwhile a 16-foot truck that's technically undersized for the same job can still park right at the door and just make a second trip.

That trade-off, smaller truck plus an extra trip versus bigger truck stuck three blocks away, comes up on Boston moves more than almost anywhere else. Keep both variables in mind as you read the sizing guide below.

## What Size Truck Fits a Studio, 1-Bedroom, or 2-Bedroom?

Here's the standard sizing breakdown, cross-checked against [U-Haul's official truck size guide](https://www.uhaul.com/Tips/Loading/How-To-Choose-The-Right-Size-Moving-Truck-Rental-15591/) and [Extra Space Storage's truck size calculator](https://www.extraspace.com/moving/tools/moving-truck-size-calculator/), then adjusted for what actually works on Boston streets.

| Home Size | Truck Length | Cubic Feet Needed | Boston Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Studio | 10 ft (or cargo van) | 200-400 cu ft | Fits almost any street, no permit hassle |
| 1-Bedroom | 10-15 ft | 400-600 cu ft | Workhorse size for triple-deckers and walk-ups |
| 2-Bedroom | 15-20 ft | 500-800 cu ft | 20 ft is the safer default once you own real furniture |
| 3-Bedroom | 20-26 ft | 900-1,300 cu ft | Check the street width before booking this size |
| 4+ Bedroom | 26 ft | 1,300+ cu ft | May need two trips on narrow blocks regardless |

Treat the low end of each range as the bare minimum, not the target. A studio with a real bed frame, a couch, and a full closet of off-season clothes and winter gear eats up more cubic footage once it's actually packed into boxes than it looked like sitting in the apartment. Renting one size up from your first guess is cheap insurance against a truck that's full at 80% loaded with a stack of boxes still on the sidewalk.

## Why Bigger Isn't Always Better on Boston Streets

Size the truck for your stuff first, then check it against your actual street. A handful of Boston neighborhoods make this check non-negotiable rather than optional.

The North End's colonial-era layout means some streets were never built for anything wider than a horse cart, let alone a 26-foot box truck. Beacon Hill adds narrow lanes on top of a real grade, which makes maneuvering a long truck slower and riskier even where it technically fits. Southie and parts of Somerville run heavy on one-way streets and resident parking, where a truck that needs to block a lane for hours draws attention fast.

None of this means avoid big trucks in Boston. It means don't default to the biggest size just because your inventory technically qualifies. If your street is tight, a mid-size truck making two trips is frequently the faster, lower-stress option, even though it looks like more work on paper.

## How Many Cubic Feet Does Your Move Actually Need?

The rule of thumb most truck-size calculators use is 150-200 cubic feet per fully furnished room, which lines up with the ranges in the table above. Walk through your place room by room and count what's actually leaving with you, not just the big furniture.

A few things people consistently under-count: closets and off-season storage (winter coats, holiday decorations, sports gear), anything currently in a basement or building storage unit, and boxes you haven't packed yet but definitely will fill. Add roughly 20% on top of your room-by-room estimate to cover this. It's a lot cheaper to rent a truck one size up than to show up short and need a second run.

## Do You Need a Parking Permit for Your Truck Size?

Usually, yes, and the size of the truck doesn't change the process much. If a truck needs to occupy street parking for more than a few minutes, most Boston neighborhoods require a no-parking permit reserving that curb space ahead of time. We've covered the [exact permit process and timing](/blog/boston-moving-truck-parking-permit) in detail elsewhere on the blog, but the short version: apply well before your move date, because last-minute applications get denied.

A bigger truck does make the permit more important, not less. A 10-foot truck can sometimes get away with a quick double-park if you're fast. A 26-footer blocking a lane without a permit is a tow risk from the moment it stops.

## What About Triple-Deckers and Walk-Ups?

Truck size and building access are two different problems that people tend to lump together. Stairs slow down loading no matter what size truck is parked outside, because the bottleneck is the walk from your door to the curb, not the truck's cargo capacity. A bigger truck doesn't move boxes down three flights any faster.

What actually helps with a walk-up is [enough people on the crew](/blog/how-many-movers-do-i-need-boston), not a bigger vehicle. If you're renting a truck yourself for a triple-decker move, size it to your inventory using the table above, and separately plan for extra hours and possibly extra hands to handle the stairs. Treating those as the same decision is where DIY moves in walk-ups tend to run long.

## Common Truck-Size Mistakes People Make

A few mistakes show up often enough to call out directly. People rent based on bedroom count alone and forget furniture density, so two people with a minimalist 2-bedroom and two people with a fully furnished 2-bedroom end up needing different trucks even though the listings look identical. People also forget to measure doorways and stairwells at the new place, not just the truck's cargo dimensions, and a couch that fit fine going out doesn't automatically fit going in.

The last one is booking a truck size based on the smallest option that could theoretically work, rather than the size that comfortably works. The price difference between a 15-foot and a 20-foot rental is usually smaller than people expect, especially against the cost of a second trip, a damaged doorframe, or furniture left on the sidewalk.
