Short answer: PODS wins on the sticker price, full-service movers usually win once Boston's streets, stairs, and permit rules enter the math. Here's how the two actually compare once you account for what a cost calculator leaves out.

## PODS vs. Full-Service Movers: The Real Cost Gap

The headline numbers are real. Per [MoveBuddha's 2026 PODS cost breakdown](https://www.movebuddha.com/blog/pods-cost/), a local PODS move runs roughly $524-$640, while a full-service local move in a market like Boston typically lands between $1,500-$2,500. For long-distance, PODS ranges from about $1,000 up to $3,500+ depending on distance and container count, against $4,000-$7,000+ for a full-service interstate move.

That's a wide gap on paper. It's also an incomplete comparison, because the PODS number is for the container and the truck that hauls it. It doesn't include anyone to load it.

## What You're Actually Paying For

Full-service pricing covers a crew that shows up, wraps your furniture, carries everything down three flights, loads the truck, drives it, and carries everything back up at the other end, all in one scheduled window. PODS pricing covers a container getting dropped off and picked up. The loading and unloading are on you, or on whoever you can talk into helping for pizza and beer.

Once you price out labor to fill and empty a PODS container, the gap shrinks fast. A three-bedroom container move plus a couple hundred dollars in day-labor loading help can land close to what a mid-tier full-service quote runs for the same job. The container isn't cheaper by the margin it looks like once somebody has to actually move the boxes into it.

Protection is the other line item people forget to price in. A moving company's coverage applies to items its own crew packs and carries. Load a container yourself, and any damage from a couch shifting during transit, or a box that wasn't stacked right, generally isn't something the container company is on the hook for. That's a real cost, even when nothing ever shows up on an invoice.

## Can You Even Put a Container on a Boston Street?

This is the part most cost comparisons skip entirely, and it's the one that matters most here. Boston requires a permit to place any portable storage container on a public street, and per the City's own [moving truck permit FAQ](https://www.boston.gov/departments/parking-clerk/moving-truck-permit-faqs), PODS containers don't go through the standard moving-truck application. Customers are directed to a separate third-party permit service, Permit Puller, to handle that specific process. Other container brands generally follow the regular truck-permit route; PODS is the outlier.

Practically, that means a PODS move in a dense Boston neighborhood involves an extra phone call and an extra layer of coordination that a same-day moving truck doesn't. Street permits also aren't issued for indefinite stretches. They're granted for a short, defined window, so a container camped on the curb for a couple of weeks while you load it isn't realistic on most blocks. If your building has a driveway or an off-street spot, none of this applies to you. If your only option is curbside on a narrow street in Allston, the South End, or Beacon Hill, you're now managing a permit process on top of the actual move. We cover the general truck-permit side of this in our [Boston moving permit guide](/blog/boston-moving-truck-parking-permit), and the short version holds here too: sort out where the truck or container legally sits before you commit to a date.

## Where PODS Genuinely Makes Sense

If you've got a driveway, a private lot, or you're moving from a suburban house with room to park a container for a few days, PODS solves a real problem: it decouples packing from moving day. You load at your own pace over a weekend instead of racing a crew's clock, and the built-in month of storage is useful if your new place isn't ready yet. For a [storage-during-a-move situation](/blog/boston-storage-options-during-a-move) where you need a gap between move-out and move-in, that flexibility is worth something a full-service truck can't offer.

It's also a reasonable call for people with the physical ability and the friends to actually do the loading, and who aren't dealing with narrow streets, walk-ups, or a hard deadline. Just go in clear-eyed about what that actually involves. Loading a 16-foot container correctly, so nothing shifts on the drive and the weight is distributed so it doesn't tip when the truck turns, takes more planning than most first-timers expect. It's a different skill than carrying boxes down a hallway.

## Where Full-Service Movers Win

Anywhere the building itself is the obstacle. Three flights in a Somerville triple-decker, a tight stairwell in a South End brownstone, or a unit with no off-street parking at all are exactly the situations where a container's footprint and permit requirements start working against you instead of for you. A same-day truck can be in, loaded, and gone in a few hours; a container has to sit somewhere, legally, for however long loading actually takes.

Full-service also wins on anything fragile, heavy, or awkward. [Furniture disassembly](/blog/furniture-disassembly-boston-move), stairs, and narrow doorways are what a trained crew is actually being paid to handle well. A rented container doesn't come with anyone who's carried a sectional up a Beacon Hill stairwell before.

## What About September 1st?

Skip the container if your move lands anywhere near [September 1st](/blog/moving-september-1-boston). Boston's peak lease-turnover date already has crews and trucks packed into every neighborhood at once, and permit windows get tighter as demand spikes. A container sitting on a curb for an extra day during that stretch is exactly when access problems, towing risk, and neighbor complaints show up. A same-day full-service move gets you in and out before any of that becomes your problem.

## So Which One Should You Book?

Run the honest math before deciding on cost alone. If you've got off-street parking, flexible timing, and people willing to load boxes, PODS can genuinely save money. If you're in a walk-up, on a narrow street, or moving on a deadline, the labor and permit costs you'd add to a container quote usually erase most of the savings anyway, and you're left doing the hard physical work yourself on top of it.

For [most local Boston moves](/local-moving), the full-service math ends up closer than the sticker price suggests, once you actually account for who's carrying the couch. A quick gut check before you book either one: if you can't picture yourself and two friends loading a fully packed container in under a day, price out the labor add-on before you compare it against a full-service quote. That's the number that actually decides which option wins.
