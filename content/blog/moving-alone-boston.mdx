Roughly three in ten U.S. households are just one person, per the [U.S. Census Bureau](https://www.census.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2023/families-and-living-arrangements.html), so if you're moving without a partner, roommate, or family nearby to help, you're not the exception. You're most of the market.

## What to DIY vs. What to Hire Out

The instinct when moving alone is to treat it as all-or-nothing: either you do everything yourself, or you book a full crew. Neither extreme fits most solo moves. The better approach is splitting the job by task, since some parts of a move don't need a second person at all, and others can't be done without one.

| Task | Solo-Friendly? | Hire Out? |
|---|---|---|
| Packing boxes, wrapping fragiles | Yes | Rarely needed |
| Disassembling furniture | Yes, with tools and time | Only if unfamiliar |
| Loading boxes and small items onto a truck | Yes, with a hand truck | No |
| Moving a couch, dresser, or mattress down stairs | Risky past one flight | Recommended |
| Loading/unloading a full truck in one day | Exhausting, doable | Recommended |
| Appliances, pianos, safes | No | Required |

Packing is where solo movers should spend most of their own labor. It's slow, but it's low-risk and doesn't need a second set of hands. Save your money for the twenty minutes where a second body actually changes the outcome.

Budget your time accordingly too. A two-person crew typically clears a studio or one-bedroom in 3-4 hours. Doing the same job alone, with no one to watch the truck or take the other end of a dresser, realistically stretches to a full day once loading, driving, and unloading are all on one set of shoulders.

## How Much Weight Can You Safely Lift Alone?

This is the number most solo-move guides skip, and it's the one that actually matters. Under ideal conditions, two-handed lifting research from the CDC's [National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/ergonomics/about/RNLE.html) puts the absolute upper bound around 51 pounds. Ideal conditions means close to your body, no twisting, a good grip, and doing it once. A moving day is the opposite of ideal conditions on every count.

Treat 35 pounds as your real ceiling for a box you're lifting alone, repeatedly, on stairs. That's lighter than a fully packed box of books or kitchen items usually weighs, which is exactly why over-packed boxes are the single most common cause of a solo-mover back injury. Bend at the knees, keep the load close to your torso, and pivot your feet instead of twisting your spine. None of that fixes a box that's simply too heavy to begin with.

Furniture is a separate problem from boxes. A dresser or a sectional isn't heavy because of raw weight, it's heavy because of shape, and shape is what a second person actually solves. You can't grip both ends of a couch alone the way two people can, no matter how strong you are.

## Boston's Walk-Ups Make Moving Alone Harder

A solo move that would be manageable in a ground-floor ranch house gets considerably harder in a Boston triple-decker. Most of the city's rental housing stock predates elevators entirely, which means three flights of narrow, turning stairs are the default in Somerville, Allston, Jamaica Plain, and a good chunk of Cambridge and Dorchester.

Stairs change the math on everything. A box that's fine to carry across a flat hallway becomes a two-handed balancing act on a stairwell with a tight landing turn, and you lose the ability to see your feet the moment you're carrying anything above chest height. [Furniture that won't clear a Boston stairwell](/blog/furniture-wont-fit-up-boston-stairs) at all is its own separate problem, but even furniture that does fit takes real coordination to get down three flights without gouging a wall or dropping something on the stairs below you.

On-street parking adds a second layer. Without someone to watch the truck while you're inside carrying a load up three flights, you're leaving a loaded moving truck unattended on a narrow street, sometimes for the length of an entire afternoon. That's a real risk in denser neighborhoods, and it's one more argument for at least a short window of paid help rather than a fully solo day.

## The Two-Week Solo Move Timeline

Working alone means everything takes longer, so the biggest mistake solo movers make is compressing the schedule to match what a two-person move would need. Give yourself two full weeks of evenings and weekends, not one frantic weekend before the truck shows up.

Two weeks out, book your truck rental or moving help and start collecting boxes. One week out, pack everything except what you use daily, label boxes by room, and disassemble large furniture ahead of time so moving day is carrying, not building. Three days out, pack the essentials box and confirm your parking permit or loading zone if you're renting a truck. The day before, do a final sweep of closets and cabinets, and load anything you can manage alone the night before so moving day itself is shorter.

This isn't dramatically different from a [full moving checklist timeline](/blog/boston-moving-checklist-timeline), but a solo mover has zero slack for a missed step, since there's no one else to catch what you forgot.

## Gear That Replaces a Second Set of Hands

A hand truck is the single highest-value purchase for a solo move, full stop. It turns a 60-pound box from a two-person lift into something you roll instead of carry, and it's the closest thing to an actual substitute for a second person that exists.

Furniture sliders do the same job for anything with feet or legs. Slide a dresser or couch across a hardwood or tile floor instead of lifting it, and a job that would strain your back becomes a push. A moving strap, worn over the shoulders, redistributes weight from your arms and back to your legs and core for anything you do have to carry, and it's genuinely the difference between managing a mattress alone and not being able to move it at all. None of this gear is expensive relative to a single hospital co-pay for a strained back, which is the actual alternative most solo movers are weighing without realizing it.

## When Should You Call In Backup?

There's a real line between "I can grind through this" and "I'm going to hurt myself or damage the building." That line is usually crossed at three specific points: anything over roughly 75 pounds, anything you can't see around or over while carrying it, and any staircase with a blind turn where you can't spot the next step.

Pianos, safes, and gun cabinets aren't solo-mover territory at any fitness level, and neither is a fully loaded dresser with the drawers still in it. If a piece of furniture fails any of those three tests, that's the moment to book help rather than push through it. Pushing through it is the single easiest way to end a solo move with an injury instead of just being tired.

Weather changes the calculation too. A piece of furniture you could manage alone on a dry, clear day gets a lot riskier on a set of ice-glazed granite steps in February, or on a stairwell slick from a summer downpour. If the forecast and the item both push toward the edge of what you can safely handle, that's two strikes, not one, and it's a reasonable point to bring in help even for something you'd normally carry yourself.

## Is It Cheaper to Hire Movers for Just the Heavy Stuff?

Usually, yes, and this is the option most solo-move guides don't mention at all. A short, [labor-only booking](/local-moving) for the one or two hours where a couch or mattress actually needs a second set of hands costs a fraction of a full-day, full-crew move, since you're only paying for the part of the job that actually needed a second person.

We see this pattern often on solo moves that start out as fully DIY: someone packs, disassembles, and loads everything themselves, then realizes at 9pm the night before that the sectional isn't going down three flights alone. That's exactly the situation a two-hour booking solves, and it's a far cheaper fix than trying to recruit a neighbor at the last minute or risking an injury to save a couple hundred dollars.
